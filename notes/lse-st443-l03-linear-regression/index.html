Recap: determinants by Gaussian elimination

The lecture opened with extra slides added to Lecture 1.

Laplace (cofactor) expansion along the first row of a 3 × 3 matrix:

det⁡(abcdefghi)=adet⁡(efhi)−bdet⁡(dfgi)+cdet⁡(degh)\det\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c\\d&e&f\\g&h&i\end{pmatrix}=a\det\begin{pmatrix}e&f\\h&i\end{pmatrix}-b\det\begin{pmatrix}d&f\\g&i\end{pmatrix}+c\det\begin{pmatrix}d&e\\g&h\end{pmatrix}det​adg​beh​cfi​​=adet(eh​fi​)−bdet(dg​fi​)+cdet(dg​eh​)

Gaussian elimination scales better than Laplace. Every row operation is multiplication by an elementary matrix with a known determinant:

  1. Swap two rows ((0110)\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}(01​10​)): det = −1, so the determinant flips sign.
  2. Scale a row by c (diag(c, 1)): det = c.
  3. Add c × one row to another ((1c01)\begin{pmatrix}1&c\\0&1\end{pmatrix}(10​c1​)): det = 1, so the determinant is unchanged.

Reduce to triangular form, multiply the diagonal, and correct for the swaps and scalings. Elimination costs about n3n^3n3 operations; naive Laplace expansion costs about n!n!n!, which is hopeless beyond n ≈ 10.

Least squares

Given IID realisations (xi,yi)i=1n(\mathbf x_i,y_i)_{i=1}^n(xi​,yi​)i=1n​, minimise the residual sum of squares:

RSS(β)=∑i[yi−β0−∑jxi,jβj]2=∥y−Xβ∥22 → min⁡β∈Rp+1\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol\beta)=\sum_{i}\Big[y_i-\beta_0-\sum_jx_{i,j}\beta_j\Big]^2=\|\mathbf y-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta\|_2^2\ \to\ \min_{\boldsymbol\beta\in\mathbb R^{p+1}}RSS(β)=i∑​[yi​−β0​−j∑​xi,j​βj​]2=∥y−Xβ∥22​ → β∈Rp+1min​

with y∈Rn\mathbf y\in\mathbb R^ny∈Rn and X\mathbf XX the n×(p+1)n\times(p+1)n×(p+1) matrix whose i-th row is (1,xiT)(1,\mathbf x_i^{\mathsf T})(1,xiT​). On the plane-fit picture, the vertical sticks are the residuals being squared.

  • RSE =RSS/(n−p−1)=\sqrt{\mathrm{RSS}/(n-p-1)}=RSS/(n−p−1)​: the RSS rescaled into the units of Y.
  • For sales ≈ β₀ + β₁ TV, RSS as a function of (β0,β1)(\beta_0,\beta_1)(β0​,β1​) is a bowl. Its contours are nested ellipses and the least squares estimate sits at the bottom.

Regressing sales (thousands of units) on TV budget (thousands of dollars) over 200 markets gives β^0=7.0325\hat\beta_0=7.0325β^​0​=7.0325, β^1=0.0475\hat\beta_1=0.0475β^​1​=0.0475: an extra $1,000 of TV spend goes with about 47.5 more units sold. What regression answers

For sales ≈ β₀ + β₁ TV + β₂ radio + β₃ newspaper:

  1. Is there any relation? H0:β=0H_0:\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf 0H0​:β=0: the F statistic.
  2. How strong? RSE and R².
  3. Which media matter? H0:βj=0H_0:\beta_j=0H0​:βj​=0 for a given j: the t-test.
  4. How large is each effect? A confidence interval for βj\beta_jβj​, or a confidence set for β\boldsymbol\betaβ.
  5. Would non-linearity help? Polynomial regression and feature maps.

Solving the least squares problem

Step 1: expand. Using ∥v∥2=vTv\|\mathbf v\|^2=\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf v∥v∥2=vTv:

RSS(β)=yTy−2yTXβ+βTXTXβ\mathrm{RSS}(\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y-2\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\boldsymbol\betaRSS(β)=yTy−2yTXβ+βTXTXβ

Careful here: the two cross terms yTXβ\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\boldsymbol\betayTXβ and βTXTy\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf yβTXTy are equal scalars (one is the transpose of the other), which is where the 2 comes from.

Step 2: differentiate, using ∂(cTa)/∂a=cT\partial(\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}\mathbf a)/\partial\mathbf a=\mathbf c^{\mathsf T}∂(cTa)/∂a=cT and ∂(aTBa)/∂a=aT(B+BT)\partial(\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}\mathbf B\mathbf a)/\partial\mathbf a=\mathbf a^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf B+\mathbf B^{\mathsf T})∂(aTBa)/∂a=aT(B+BT), with B=XTX\mathbf B=\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf XB=XTX symmetric:

∂RSS∂β=−2yTX+2βTXTX=−2(yT−βTXT)X\frac{\partial\mathrm{RSS}}{\partial\boldsymbol\beta}=-2\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X+2\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X=-2(\mathbf y^{\mathsf T}-\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T})\mathbf X∂β∂RSS​=−2yTX+2βTXTX=−2(yT−βTXT)X

Step 3: the Hessian.

∂2RSS∂β ∂βT=2XTX⪰0\frac{\partial^2\mathrm{RSS}}{\partial\boldsymbol\beta\,\partial\boldsymbol\beta^{\mathsf T}}=2\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\succeq0∂β∂βT∂2RSS​=2XTX⪰0

It is positive semidefinite because vTXTXv=∥Xv∥2≥0\mathbf v^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\mathbf v=\|\mathbf X\mathbf v\|^2\ge0vTXTXv=∥Xv∥2≥0, i.e. it can be rewritten as an inner product. For a matrix the right condition is positive semidefinite, not ”> 0”: a PSD Hessian everywhere makes RSS convex, so any point where the gradient vanishes is a global minimum. Positive definite (X of full column rank) also makes it unique.

Step 4: normal equations. Set the gradient to zero:

XT(y−Xβ)=0  ⟺  XTXβ=XTy\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}(\mathbf y-\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta)=\mathbf 0\iff\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta=\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf yXT(y−Xβ)=0⟺XTXβ=XTy

Each column of X has zero inner product with the residual vector, so the residual is orthogonal to the columns of X.

Step 5: solve. If X has full column rank, (XTX)−1(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}(XTX)−1 exists:

β^=(XTX)−1XTy,y^=Xβ^=X(XTX)−1XT⏟=:Hy\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf y,\qquad \hat{\mathbf y}=\mathbf X\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}=\underbrace{\mathbf X(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}}_{=:\mathbf H}\mathbf yβ^​=(XTX)−1XTy,y^​=Xβ^​==:HX(XTX)−1XT​​y

(XTX)−1XT(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}(XTX)−1XT is the pseudo-inverse, and H is the hat matrix: it puts the hat on y. Prediction at a new point is f^(x0)=(1,x0T)β^\hat f(\mathbf x_0)=(1,\mathbf x_0^{\mathsf T})\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}f^​(x0​)=(1,x0T​)β^​.

y^\hat{\mathbf y}y^​ is the point in the column space of X closest to y, and the residual y−y^\mathbf y-\hat{\mathbf y}y−y^​ drops perpendicularly onto it. H is the projection matrix; an orthogonal projection satisfies P2=P=PT\mathbf P^2=\mathbf P=\mathbf P^{\mathsf T}P2=P=PT, and for H, H2=X(XTX)−1XTX(XTX)−1XT=H\mathbf H^2=\mathbf X(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}=\mathbf HH2=X(XTX)−1XTX(XTX)−1XT=H. Projecting twice changes nothing.

When (XTX)−1(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}(XTX)−1 does not exist (linearly dependent columns, a duplicated predictor, or p + 1 > n):

  • β^\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}β^​ might not be unique; infinitely many solve the normal equations.
  • y^\hat{\mathbf y}y^​ is still unique, because the projection onto the column space does not depend on which combination spans it.
  • Software usually detects it and drops a column or reports NA.

Note that XTX\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf XXTX is always positive semidefinite. Non-invertible means it is not positive definite: some v≠0\mathbf v\ne\mathbf 0v=0 has Xv=0\mathbf X\mathbf v=\mathbf 0Xv=0, so it has a zero eigenvalue.

Distribution of the estimator (fixed design)

With X fixed, y=Xβ+ϵ\mathbf y=\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta+\boldsymbol\epsilony=Xβ+ϵ and ϵ∼N(0,σ2I)\boldsymbol\epsilon\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2\mathbf I)ϵ∼N(0,σ2I), so y∼N(Xβ,σ2I)\mathbf y\sim\mathcal N(\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta,\sigma^2\mathbf I)y∼N(Xβ,σ2I). Apply the affine rule with A=(XTX)−1XT\mathbf A=(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}A=(XTX)−1XT:

  1. Mean: (XTX)−1XTXβ=β(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X\boldsymbol\beta=\boldsymbol\beta(XTX)−1XTXβ=β, so β^\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}β^​ is unbiased.
  2. Covariance: A(σ2I)AT=(XTX)−1σ2\mathbf A(\sigma^2\mathbf I)\mathbf A^{\mathsf T}=(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\sigma^2A(σ2I)AT=(XTX)−1σ2.
β^∼N(β, (XTX)−1σ2)\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}\sim\mathcal N\big(\boldsymbol\beta,\ (\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\sigma^2\big)β^​∼N(β, (XTX)−1σ2)

Watch for the covariance written as (XXT)−1σ2(\mathbf X\mathbf X^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\sigma^2(XXT)−1σ2. That matrix is n × n and usually singular; the right one is the (p+1) × (p+1) matrix above. Also, this is not the Gauss–Markov (BLUE) result: Gauss–Markov says OLS is the best linear unbiased estimator under uncorrelated, equal-variance errors without assuming normality. Normal errors give the stronger statement here, the exact sampling distribution.

Why an ellipse: its axes are the eigenvectors of XTX\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf XXTX. If two predictors are highly correlated, XTX\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf XXTX has a small eigenvalue along their difference and the ellipse is long and thin in that direction. The data pin down the sum of their effects but not how to split it, which is the newspaper and radio story above in geometric form.

TSS is the total variability in the response and RSS what is left after the regression, so R² is the share of the variability in Y explained by X. For p = 1, R2=r2R^2=r^2R2=r2, the squared sample correlation.

Linear regression versus KNN regression

Linear regressionKNN regression
Typeparametricnon-parametric
Proseasy to fit and interpret, tests availableflexible, no shape assumed
Consforces a linear fneeds many points near x0x_0x0​

Given K and a prediction point x0x_0x0​, let N0\mathcal N_0N0​ be the K training points nearest to x0x_0x0​ and average their responses:

f^(x0)=1K∑xi∈N0yi\hat f(x_0)=\frac1K\sum_{x_i\in\mathcal N_0}y_if^​(x0​)=K1​xi​∈N0​∑​yi​

There is no forced degree and K can be anything. With K = 3 and neighbours y = 2.0, 2.6, 3.1, f^(x0)=7.7/3=2.567\hat f(x_0)=7.7/3=2.567f^​(x0​)=7.7/3=2.567. Another point has a different set of three nearest neighbours, because N0\mathcal N_0N0​ is recomputed for every query, which is why the fitted surface is a staircase. KNN gives no β’s to test, so it is less interpretable: it just predicts y0y_0y0​ as f^(x0)\hat f(x_0)f^​(x0​).

  • K = 1 is rough (low bias, high variance); K = 9 is smoother (more bias, less variance).
  • If the truth is exactly linear, linear regression wins for every K. Slightly non-linear: KNN only edges ahead for K ≥ 4. Strongly non-linear: KNN wins for every K shown.
  • KNN gets harder as the input dimension grows, because neighbours stop being near: the curse of dimensionality.

How fast neighbourhoods empty out: with data uniform on [0,1]p[0,1]^p[0,1]p, a cube holding 10% of the points needs edge 0.11/p0.1^{1/p}0.11/p, which is 0.10 for p = 1, 0.32 for p = 2 and 0.79 for p = 10. At p = 10 the “nearest” 10% span 79% of each axis.

Kernels do not cure this: kernel smoothing is a weighted KNN and needs local data just as much. What helps is imposing structure: a parametric or additive model, variable selection and regularisation, or dimension reduction. If a few eigenvalues of the covariance matrix carry most of the variance, the data effectively live in fewer than p dimensions, which is the idea behind PCA.

ISLR §3.5. With 50 training points, KNN beats linear regression on a non-linear truth when p = 1 or 2, but for p ≥ 3 or 4 linear regression wins, because 50 points spread over many dimensions leave no near neighbours. Parametric methods tend to win when there are few observations per predictor.

Traps

  • Writing the covariance of β^\hat{\boldsymbol\beta}β^​ as (XXT)−1σ2(\mathbf X\mathbf X^{\mathsf T})^{-1}\sigma^2(XXT)−1σ2; it is (XTX)−1σ2(\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf X)^{-1}\sigma^2(XTX)−1σ2.
  • Saying a non-invertible XTX\mathbf X^{\mathsf T}\mathbf XXTX is “not PSD”: it is always PSD, it just fails to be PD.
  • “Hessian > 0” for a matrix: say PSD (convex, global minimum) or PD (unique minimum).
  • xˉ\bar xxˉ instead of xˉ 2\bar x^{\,2}xˉ2 in SE(β^0)\mathrm{SE}(\hat\beta_0)SE(β^​0​).
  • Forgetting the absolute value in a two-sided p-value.
  • Dividing RSS by n instead of n − p − 1.
  • Calling a joint confidence region an interval.
  • Saying OLS projects onto a space spanned by Σ: it projects onto the column space of X.
  • Expecting kernels to remove the curse of dimensionality for local methods.
  • Reading a significant simple-regression coefficient as significant in the multiple regression (newspaper).
  • Judging a model by R² alone when adding predictors.
