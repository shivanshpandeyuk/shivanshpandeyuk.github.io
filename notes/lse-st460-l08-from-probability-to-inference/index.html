Inference reverses the direction of a probability model

Probability: start with a model and ask what data may occur, model → distribution of data. Inference: observe data and reason about an unknown target, observed data → information about an estimand.

  • The reverse problem is meaningful only after the target and assumptions are explicit.
  • This is the move from probability theory to statistical inference. L1 to L7 built the probability toolkit (laws, LLN, CLT, delta method); from here it is used to learn about θ.

Start with how the observations came to exist

target population → sampling → measurement → observed data. For experiments also record how treatments were assigned.

  • Sampling can introduce selection and dependence; measurement can introduce error; missingness can enter at several stages.
  • The probability model should reflect how the data were obtained and the assumptions made about that process.

A statistical model is a family of distributions

P={Pθ:θ∈Θ}\mathcal P=\{P_\theta:\theta\in\Theta\}P={Pθ​:θ∈Θ}
  • 𝒫 is the statistical model, PθP_\thetaPθ​ the distribution indexed by θ, and Θ, the range of inputs, is the parameter space.
  • Parametric model: θ finite-dimensional. Correctly specified ⇒ the data follow Pθ0P_{\theta_0}Pθ0​​ for some unknown θ0∈Θ\theta_0\in\Thetaθ0​∈Θ.
  • Examples for one observation: {Bernoulli(p): 0 ≤ p ≤ 1}, all Bernoulli distributions with p ∈ [0, 1], and {N(μ, σ²): μ ∈ ℝ, σ > 0}, all univariate normal distributions.
  • For a sample, also specify the dependence structure, e.g. IID.

Model parameters versus estimands

A model parameter θ indexes the distributions. An estimand is the quantity we want to learn, the target, written ψ(θ): a component, or a function of several components.

  • Example. Y ∼ N(μ, σ²), θ = (μ, σ²). Estimating the mean: estimand μ, nuisance σ². Estimating the variance: estimand σ², nuisance μ.
  • “Nuisance” means outside the target of interest; it does not mean unimportant.

One Bernoulli model, several estimands. Y1,…,Yn∼iidBernoulli(p)Y_1,\dots,Y_n\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{Bernoulli}(p)Y1​,…,Yn​∼iidBernoulli(p), 0 < p < 1:

  • success probability ψ1(p)=p\psi_1(p)=pψ1​(p)=p;
  • odds ψ2(p)=p/(1−p)\psi_2(p)=p/(1-p)ψ2​(p)=p/(1−p);
  • probability of at least 10 successes in 20 future independent trials, ψ3(p)=Pp(S20≥10)\psi_3(p)=\mathbb P_p(S_{20}\ge10)ψ3​(p)=Pp​(S20​≥10), S20∼Bin(20,p)S_{20}\sim\text{Bin}(20,p)S20​∼Bin(20,p).

Same model, different targets; how we estimate and report uncertainty depends on the target (L7’s delta method converts the SE of p^\hat pp^​ into SEs for ψ2\psi_2ψ2​ and ψ3\psi_3ψ3​).

Same idea in coverage work. One model of a company’s monthly churn rate p supports several estimands: p itself, expected customer lifetime 1/p, or the probability net retention falls below a threshold next year. Each needs its own SE; 1/p in particular inherits a much wider interval when p is small (delta method: ∣g′(p)∣=1/p2|g'(p)|=1/p^2∣g′(p)∣=1/p2).

Likelihood: vary the parameter, hold the data fixed

For observed data y=(y1,…,yn)\boldsymbol y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)y=(y1​,…,yn​) with joint density or PMF fθ(n)f_\theta^{(n)}fθ(n)​:

L(θ;y)=fθ(n)(y)  viewed as a function of θ,IID: L(θ;y)=∏i=1nfθ(yi).L(\theta;\boldsymbol y)=f_\theta^{(n)}(\boldsymbol y)\ \text{ viewed as a function of }\theta,\qquad \text{IID: } L(\theta;\boldsymbol y)=\prod_{i=1}^nf_\theta(y_i).L(θ;y)=fθ(n)​(y)  viewed as a function of θ,IID: L(θ;y)=i=1∏n​fθ​(yi​).
  • Likelihood and density are not the same. The likelihood is a function of θ with a fixed, particular y; the density is a function of y, so y is not fixed. Under IID the product form is immediate.
  • Likelihood compares how well parameter values explain the observed data. It is not a probability distribution over the parameter (it need not integrate to 1 over θ).

Bernoulli likelihood.

  1. Probability mass function for one observation: P(Y=y)=py(1−p)1−y\mathbb P(Y=y)=p^y(1-p)^{1-y}P(Y=y)=py(1−p)1−y, y ∈ {0, 1}.
  2. The likelihood of p is the joint probability of the observed sequence:
L(p;y1,…,yn)=P(Y1=y1,…,Yn=yn)=iid∏i=1npyi(1−p)1−yi.L(p;y_1,\dots,y_n)=\mathbb P(Y_1=y_1,\dots,Y_n=y_n)\overset{\text{iid}}{=}\prod_{i=1}^np^{y_i}(1-p)^{1-y_i}.L(p;y1​,…,yn​)=P(Y1​=y1​,…,Yn​=yn​)=iidi=1∏n​pyi​(1−p)1−yi​.
  1. Collect exponents:
L(p;y)=p∑yi(1−p) n−∑yi=ps(1−p)n−s.L(p;\boldsymbol y)=p^{\sum y_i}(1-p)^{\,n-\sum y_i}=p^s(1-p)^{n-s}.L(p;y)=p∑yi​(1−p)n−∑yi​=ps(1−p)n−s.
  • The likelihood says how probable the observed data are if the success rate were p.
  • For fixed n, L depends on the data only through s. Under the IID Bernoulli model, the ordering adds no information about p once s is known. (This is sufficiency, L10.)

Activity 8.1: compare the likelihood at two values

Ordered IID sample y = (1, 0, 1), so L(p;y)=p2(1−p)L(p;\boldsymbol y)=p^2(1-p)L(p;y)=p2(1−p). Compute L(0.75)/L(0.50)L(0.75)/L(0.50)L(0.75)/L(0.50); does it give the probability that p = 0.75?

  1. L(0.75)=0.752×0.25=0.140625L(0.75)=0.75^2\times0.25=0.140625L(0.75)=0.752×0.25=0.140625.
  2. L(0.50)=0.52×0.5=0.125L(0.50)=0.5^2\times0.5=0.125L(0.50)=0.52×0.5=0.125.
  3. Ratio =0.140625/0.125=9/8=1.125=0.140625/0.125=9/8=1.125=0.140625/0.125=9/8=1.125.
  4. Interpretation. The observed data are 1.125 times as probable under p = 0.75 as under p = 0.50: p = 0.75 is a slightly better explanation of the observed data.
  5. No. It is a ratio of probabilities of the data under two hypotheses, not of probabilities of p. In the frequentist model p is a fixed unknown constant; a probability for “p = 0.75” needs a prior (L17).

Note that a ratio of 1.125 means the likelihood is 12.5% higher, and the safe phrasing is “the data are 12.5% more probable under p = 0.75”, not “p = 0.75 is more likely”. Also, p2(1−p)p^2(1-p)p2(1−p) is the probability of the ordered sequence (1, 0, 1); P(2 successes in 3)=3p2(1−p)\mathbb P(2\text{ successes in }3)=3p^2(1-p)P(2 successes in 3)=3p2(1−p) (binomial). The factor 3 cancels in the ratio, so 1.125 is unaffected.

Identifiability: can the data distribution distinguish parameters?

θ identifiable if Pθ1=Pθ2⇒θ1=θ2;ψ(θ) identifiable if Pθ1=Pθ2⇒ψ(θ1)=ψ(θ2).\theta\text{ identifiable if }P_{\theta_1}=P_{\theta_2}\Rightarrow\theta_1=\theta_2;\qquad \psi(\theta)\text{ identifiable if }P_{\theta_1}=P_{\theta_2}\Rightarrow\psi(\theta_1)=\psi(\theta_2).θ identifiable if Pθ1​​=Pθ2​​⇒θ1​=θ2​;ψ(θ) identifiable if Pθ1​​=Pθ2​​⇒ψ(θ1​)=ψ(θ2​).
  • Two different parameter values must produce different data distributions; otherwise no amount of data can tell them apart.
  • The observable law may determine a target without determining the whole parameter. Identifiability does not guarantee accurate estimation from a finite sample.

Activity 8.3: which targets can a Poisson identify?

Yi∼iidPoisson(α+β)Y_i\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\text{Poisson}(\alpha+\beta)Yi​∼iidPoisson(α+β), α, β > 0. Targets α + β, α/(α + β), P(Yi=0)\mathbb P(Y_i=0)P(Yi​=0); compare (α, β) = (1, 3) and (2, 2).

  1. Both pairs give λ = α + β = 4, so both give the same law Poisson(4).
  2. α + β: identifiable. Equal laws ⇒ equal λ ⇒ equal α + β.
  3. α/(α + β): not identifiable. (1, 3) gives 1/4, (2, 2) gives 2/4, yet the laws are identical.
  4. P(Y=0)=e−(α+β)\mathbb P(Y=0)=e^{-(\alpha+\beta)}P(Y=0)=e−(α+β): identifiable, as a function of λ alone. At λ = 4 it is e−4≈0.0183e^{-4}\approx0.0183e−4≈0.0183.

The data cannot distinguish pairs with the same sum, because λ = α + β.

The data can identify a product without its factors. Y ∼ N(αβ, 1), α, β ∈ ℝ: the law depends on (α, β) only through η = αβ. (1, 2), (2, 1) and (−1, −2) give the same distribution, so η is identifiable while α and β separately are not.

Nuisance parameters still drive uncertainty

X1,…,Xn∼iidN(μ,σ2)X_1,\dots,X_n\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)X1​,…,Xn​∼iidN(μ,σ2), target μ, nuisance σ². But

SE(Xˉ)=σn.\mathrm{SE}(\bar X)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}.SE(Xˉ)=n​σ​.

For inference (e.g. how close the sample mean is to the population mean) the answer depends on σ. Hence the need to estimate σ and studentise (L7, Slutsky).

The running regression model

Conditional on the entire design X = x:

Yi=α+βxi+εi,(ε1,…,εn)⊤∣X=x∼Nn(0,σ2In),Y_i=\alpha+\beta x_i+\varepsilon_i,\qquad(\varepsilon_1,\dots,\varepsilon_n)^\top\mid\boldsymbol X=\boldsymbol x\sim\mathcal N_n(\mathbf 0,\sigma^2I_n),Yi​=α+βxi​+εi​,(ε1​,…,εn​)⊤∣X=x∼Nn​(0,σ2In​),

equivalently Yi∣X=x∼N(α+βxi,σ2)Y_i\mid\boldsymbol X=\boldsymbol x\sim\mathcal N(\alpha+\beta x_i,\sigma^2)Yi​∣X=x∼N(α+βxi​,σ2) independently. Parameter vector (α, β, σ²).

  • Each YiY_iYi​ depends on xix_ixi​, so the YiY_iYi​ are independent but not identically distributed: same variance, different means.
  • For random predictors, “errors IID” without conditioning on the predictors does not ensure this conditional model.

Slope interpretation. Under E[Y∣X=x]=α+βx\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]=\alpha+\beta xE[Y∣X=x]=α+βx, E[Y∣X=x+1]−E[Y∣X=x]=β\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x+1]-\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]=\betaE[Y∣X=x+1]−E[Y∣X=x]=β: β compares conditional means one predictor unit apart, within the range where the model applies.

  • Identifying α and β separately needs at least two distinct design values; if every xi=x0x_i=x_0xi​=x0​, only α+βx0\alpha+\beta x_0α+βx0​ is identifiable.
  • Reading β as the effect of changing X needs design or causal assumptions (see the design section below).

The regression likelihood makes its assumptions visible

  1. Conditional density of one response:
fY∣X=x(y)=1σ2πexp⁡{−(y−α−βx)22σ2}.f_{Y\mid X=x}(y)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(y-\alpha-\beta x)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}.fY∣X=x​(y)=σ2π​1​exp{−2σ2(y−α−βx)2​}.
  1. Conditional independence ⇒ product over i:
L(α,β,σ2;y)=∏i=1nfY∣X=xi(yi)=∏i=1n1σ2πexp⁡{−(yi−α−βxi)22σ2}.L(\alpha,\beta,\sigma^2;\boldsymbol y)=\prod_{i=1}^nf_{Y\mid X=x_i}(y_i)=\prod_{i=1}^n\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}\exp\Big\{-\frac{(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2}{2\sigma^2}\Big\}.L(α,β,σ2;y)=i=1∏n​fY∣X=xi​​(yi​)=i=1∏n​σ2π​1​exp{−2σ2(yi​−α−βxi​)2​}.
  1. Take logs:
ℓ=log⁡L=−n2log⁡(2πσ2)−12σ2∑i=1n(yi−α−βxi)2.\ell=\log L=-\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2)-\frac1{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\alpha-\beta x_i)^2.ℓ=logL=−2n​log(2πσ2)−2σ21​i=1∑n​(yi​−α−βxi​)2.
  1. For fixed σ² > 0 the first term is constant in (α, β), and the second is −12σ2×-\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\times−2σ21​×RSS, so maximising ℓ ⇔ minimising the residual sum of squares.

The likelihood encodes linear conditional means, common conditional variance, normal errors and conditional independence. Each assumption is visible in a factor of the formula.

Fit the centred regression model

Design x = (−1, 0, 1), σ² > 0 fixed. Minimise

Q(a,b)=(y1−a+b)2+(y2−a)2+(y3−a−b)2.Q(a,b)=(y_1-a+b)^2+(y_2-a)^2+(y_3-a-b)^2.Q(a,b)=(y1​−a+b)2+(y2​−a)2+(y3​−a−b)2.

Normal equations.

  1. ∂Q/∂a=−2(y1−a+b)−2(y2−a)−2(y3−a−b)=−2(y1+y2+y3−3a)\partial Q/\partial a=-2(y_1-a+b)-2(y_2-a)-2(y_3-a-b)=-2(y_1+y_2+y_3-3a)∂Q/∂a=−2(y1​−a+b)−2(y2​−a)−2(y3​−a−b)=−2(y1​+y2​+y3​−3a); the b terms cancel because ∑xi=0\sum x_i=0∑xi​=0. Set to 0: 3α^=y1+y2+y33\hat\alpha=y_1+y_2+y_33α^=y1​+y2​+y3​.
  2. ∂Q/∂b=2(y1−a+b)−2(y3−a−b)=2(y1−y3+2b)\partial Q/\partial b=2(y_1-a+b)-2(y_3-a-b)=2(y_1-y_3+2b)∂Q/∂b=2(y1​−a+b)−2(y3​−a−b)=2(y1​−y3​+2b). Set to 0: 2β^=y3−y12\hat\beta=y_3-y_12β^​=y3​−y1​.

Centring (∑xi=0\sum x_i=0∑xi​=0) is what decouples the two equations.

Activity 8.2: compute and interpret

y = (2, 1, 6).

  1. α^=(2+1+6)/3=3\hat\alpha=(2+1+6)/3=3α^=(2+1+6)/3=3.
  2. β^=(6−2)/2=2\hat\beta=(6-2)/2=2β^​=(6−2)/2=2.
  3. Conditional-means reading. α^=3\hat\alpha=3α^=3 is the estimated mean response at x = 0 (the centre of the design). β^=2\hat\beta=2β^​=2: groups one predictor unit apart differ by an estimated 2 in mean response; fitted means at x = −1, 0, 1 are 1, 3, 5.
  4. Causal reading would need, for instance, x to be assigned at random (or a credible no-confounding argument), plus the linear form holding over the range considered. The regression alone only compares observed groups.

Design, targets and representativeness

  • Fixed design: predictor values set in advance; repeated sampling holds them fixed. Random design: pairs (Xi,Yi)(X_i,Y_i)(Xi​,Yi​) sampled, so both vary. With random design we can still condition on observed x; the same conditional likelihood applies without modelling X.
  • Random sampling and random treatment assignment are different design choices. A causal reading of the slope needs appropriate design or causal assumptions.
  • Three targets: descriptive (how does mean score vary with observed study time?), predictive (what score should we predict for a new student?), causal (how would the mean score change if an intervention increased study time?). Observed differences do not, by themselves, identify the effect of extra study.
  • Selection: with S = 1 for inclusion, E[Y∣X=x,S=1]\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x,S=1]E[Y∣X=x,S=1] may differ from E[Y∣X=x]\mathbb E[Y\mid X=x]E[Y∣X=x]. Income survey example: if higher earners within an age group respond less, respondents’ mean income understates the age group’s. A larger sample estimates the participants’ mean more precisely without making it representative.
  • A straight line can summarise a curve: with IID pairs and suitable conditions, least squares estimates the best linear predictor in the sampled population, which depends on how common different X values are.
  • Robust SEs give valid large-sample uncertainty under unequal conditional variances. They leave the fitted line unchanged and do not remove selection bias or establish causality.

Formulate the problem before choosing a method

  1. Estimand: what quantity or comparison?
  2. Data collection: sampling, assignment, measurement.
  3. Model: distributions and dependence.
  4. Identifiability: does the observed-data law determine the estimand?
  5. Estimation: which estimator or fitting rule?
  6. Uncertainty: which SEs or intervals, and why?

Then ask whether the estimates answer the original question about the intended population.

Summary

  • A statistical model specifies possible distributions for the observed data.
  • The estimand expresses the question; nuisance parameters can still affect uncertainty.
  • Likelihood holds the observed data fixed and compares parameter values.
  • The estimand must be identifiable for consistent estimation across the model.
  • A regression likelihood needs assumptions beyond the conditional mean.
  • Descriptive, predictive and causal targets need different assumptions.
  • Next (L9): estimators judged by loss, risk, bias, variance and consistency.

Traps

  • Treating the likelihood as a probability distribution over θ, or a likelihood ratio as “how much more probable p = 0.75 is”.
  • Ratio arithmetic: 1.125 is 12.5% higher, not 17.5%.
  • Confusing P(sequence)=p2(1−p)\mathbb P(\text{sequence})=p^2(1-p)P(sequence)=p2(1−p) with P(2 successes)=3p2(1−p)\mathbb P(2\text{ successes})=3p^2(1-p)P(2 successes)=3p2(1−p) (harmless in ratios, wrong elsewhere).
  • Calling a parameter identifiable because it is “in the model”; check whether equal laws force equal values.
  • Thinking nuisance means ignorable: σ sets the SE of Xˉ\bar XXˉ.
  • Assuming the YiY_iYi​ in regression are identically distributed (they are not, given different xix_ixi​).
  • Reading a regression slope causally from observational data, or believing robust SEs fix bias.
  • Believing a bigger sample cures non-representative selection.
