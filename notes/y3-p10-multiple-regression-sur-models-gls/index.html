Estimating systems of equations with correlated errors using generalised least squares.

You have multiple regression equations that are seemingly unrelated (SUR). What to do?

  • GLS is only useful if (i) there is covariance in the error terms of the multiple regression equations (common shocks), and (ii) if there is not the same explanatory variables being used in both regression equations.
    • Otherwise, if you do not have (i) then you should just do separate OLS estimations
    • Otherwise, if you have (i) but do not have (ii) then that is a VAR model

Lecture Notes

Many regression equations

Setup

We are looking at M explanatory variables, y1y_1y1​ to yMy_MyM​, these are time series. They each depend on a vector of regressors, their slopes and error terms that are white noise, homoskedastic.

Two Cases

  1. The error terms across u1,u2,…umu_1, u_2, \dots u_mu1​,u2​,…um​ DO NOT covary

    • There are no correlations across the data and no gain in system estimation.
    • Each equation is a classical regression and is efficiently estimated by OLS. We know they don’t covary because the variance-covariance matrix is diagonal
    Σ=E(utut′)\Sigma = E(\bold u_t \bold u_t')Σ=E(ut​ut′​)

    E.g. consider a two variable case then

    E(utut′)=Σ=(σ11σ12σ21σ22)E(\mathbf{u}_t \mathbf{u}_t') = \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{11} & \sigma_{12} \\ \sigma_{21} & \sigma_{22} \end{pmatrix}E(ut​ut′​)=Σ=(σ11​σ21​​σ12​σ22​​)
    • Diagonal (σ11,σ22\sigma_{11}, \sigma_{22}σ11​,σ22​): These are the standard variances of each individual company’s errors.
    • Off-Diagonal (σ12\sigma_{12}σ12​): This is the Covariance, in a diagonal matrix, off-diagonal values are zero; the errors are unrelated to each other,

  2. The error terms across u1,u2,…umu_1, u_2, \dots u_mu1​,u2​,…um​ DO covary For example suppose that y1y_1y1​ is Ford stock price and y2y_2y2​ is Chrysler stock price. Both you can imagine are affected by the same ‘shocks’ such as tarriffs, thus you would say their error terms are actually related:

    In which case, this matrix is not diagonal anymore:

    E(utut′)=Σ=(σ11σ12σ21σ22)E(\mathbf{u}_t \mathbf{u}_t') = \Sigma = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{11} & \sigma_{12} \\ \sigma_{21} & \sigma_{22} \end{pmatrix}E(ut​ut′​)=Σ=(σ11​σ21​​σ12​σ22​​)

    We have, *Seemingly *Unrelated Regression equations and there may be gains to system estimation.

    GLS will be more efficient because it takes into account potential correlation between the ujtu_{jt}ujt​

GLS method for SUR

When does SUR/GLS provide a benefit?

Even if error terms covary, there are specific mathematical conditions that determine whether you get a more precise (“efficient”) result using the system approach.

Case A: No Gain (SUR = OLS) GLS is mathematically equivalent to running simple OLS on each equation separately if all equations use the exact same regressors (xit=xtx_{it} = x_txit​=xt​ for all iii).

  • Example: If you are regressing the returns of Ford, Chrysler, and GM all against just the “Market Return” and “Interest Rates,” OLS is already as efficient as it can be.
  • Note: This is often the case in VAR (Vector Autoregression) models, where every equation uses the same lagged variables

**Case B: Efficiency Gains (SUR > OLS)
**If the regressors differ between the equations, then system estimation provides efficiency gains.

  • E.g., suppose ford equation (y1) depends on truck sales data (x1)
  • E.g., suppose Chrysler equation (y2) depends on sedan tax incentives (x2)

The Logic: Because the variables are different, the model can use the “extra info” (σ12\sigma_{12}σ12​) in the error correlation to sharpen the estimates of the slopes. It essentially uses the fact that the two companies share a common “shock” to better isolate the specific impact of their unique variables.

Step 1: Stack the vectors, Y,β,uY, \beta, uY,β,u

We stack the dependent variables, coefficients, and errors into long vertical vectors:

Y=(Y1Y2⋮YM),β=(β1β2⋮βM),u=(u1u2⋮uM)Y = \begin{pmatrix} Y_1 \\ Y_2 \\ \vdots \\ Y_M \end{pmatrix}, \quad \beta = \begin{pmatrix} \beta_1 \\ \beta_2 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_M \end{pmatrix}, \quad u = \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_M \end{pmatrix}Y=​Y1​Y2​⋮YM​​​,β=​β1​β2​⋮βM​​​,u=​u1​u2​⋮uM​​​
  • YiY_iYi​: The full time series (length TTT) for asset iii, same for the others
    • So e.g. Y1=(y11,y12,…y1T)′Y_1 = (y_{11}, y_{12}, \dots y_{1T})'Y1​=(y11​,y12​,…y1T​)′

So if you write this explicitly, it actually is a massive MTx1 vector:

Y=(Y1Y2⋮YM)=(y11⋮y1Ty21⋮y2T⋮yM1⋮yMT)Y = \begin{pmatrix} Y_1 \\ Y_2 \\ \vdots \\ Y_M \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} y_{11} \\ \vdots \\ y_{1T} \\ \hline y_{21} \\ \vdots \\ y_{2T} \\ \hline \vdots \\ \hline y_{M1} \\ \vdots \\ y_{MT} \end{pmatrix}Y=​Y1​Y2​⋮YM​​​=​y11​⋮y1T​y21​⋮y2T​⋮yM1​⋮yMT​​​​

Same goes for error terms also an MTx1 vector:

u=(u1u2⋮uM)=(u11⋮u1Tu21⋮u2T⋮uM1⋮uMT)u = \begin{pmatrix} u_1 \\ u_2 \\ \vdots \\ u_M \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} u_{11} \\ \vdots \\ u_{1T} \\ \hline u_{21} \\ \vdots \\ u_{2T} \\ \hline \vdots \\ \hline u_{M1} \\ \vdots \\ u_{MT} \end{pmatrix}u=​u1​u2​⋮uM​​​=​u11​⋮u1T​u21​⋮u2T​⋮uM1​⋮uMT​​​​

Finally, we have a beta for every regressor so that β\betaβ is a Kx1 matrix.

Step 2: Block Diagonal Regressor Matrix, XXX

The regressors we arrange into a block-diagonal matrix, XXX:

X=(X10…00X2…0⋮⋮⋱⋮00…XM)X = \begin{pmatrix} X_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & X_2 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & X_M \end{pmatrix}X=​X1​0⋮0​0X2​⋮0​……⋱…​00⋮XM​​​
  • Why the zeros? The zeros ensure that the variables for Ford (X1X_1X1​) only help explain Ford’s price (Y1Y_1Y1​), while allowing us to solve for all stocks in a single equation: Y=Xβ+uY = X\beta + uY=Xβ+u

If you write this explicitly, it is also a massive matrix, we are stacking the regressor matrices for each variable’s set of observations at time t =1 to t = T.

X=(X10…00X2…0⋮⋮⋱⋮00…XM)=(x11′…0⋮⋱⋮x1T′…00x21′…⋮⋮⋱0x2T′…⋮⋮⋮0…xM1′⋮⋱⋮0…xMT′)X = \begin{pmatrix} X_1 & 0 & \dots & 0 \\ 0 & X_2 & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & X_M \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} x_{11}' & \dots & 0 \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ x_{1T}' & \dots & 0 \\ \hline 0 & x_{21}' & \dots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \\ 0 & x_{2T}' & \dots \\ \hline \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ \hline 0 & \dots & x_{M1}' \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & \dots & x_{MT}' \end{pmatrix}X=​X1​0⋮0​0X2​⋮0​……⋱…​00⋮XM​​​=​x11′​⋮x1T′​0⋮0⋮0⋮0​…⋱…x21′​⋮x2T′​⋮…⋱…​0⋮0…⋱…⋮xM1′​⋮xMT′​​​​

This creates an (MT×K)(MT \times K)(MT×K) matrix, where K=∑i=1MkiK = \sum_{i=1}^{M} k_iK=∑i=1M​ki​

Step 3: Write the matrix equation model

The system is now written as:

Y⏟(MT×1)=X⏟(MT×K)⋅β⏟(K×1)+u⏟(MT×1)\underbrace{Y}_{(MT \times 1)} = \underbrace{X}_{(MT \times K)} \cdot \underbrace{\beta}_{(K \times 1)} + \underbrace{u}_{(MT \times 1)}(MT×1)Y​​=(MT×K)X​​⋅(K×1)β​​+(MT×1)u​​
  • M = number of equations
  • T = time periods under observation
  • K = total number of regressors, across all equations ∑ki\sum k_i∑ki​

Step 4: Find the system variance-covariance matrix Ω\OmegaΩ

Recall that in a GLS, we need to weight each observation by its covariance, for this system, we write the system variance-covariance matrix. A “master map” of how every error in the system relates to every other error.

This is the MT × MT matrix Ω\OmegaΩ:

E(uu′)=Ω=(σ11ITσ12IT…σ1MITσ21ITσ22IT…σ2MIT⋮⋮⋱⋮σM1ITσM2IT…σMMIT)E(uu') = \Omega = \begin{pmatrix} \sigma_{11}I_T & \sigma_{12}I_T & \dots & \sigma_{1M}I_T \\ \sigma_{21}I_T & \sigma_{22}I_T & \dots & \sigma_{2M}I_T \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ \sigma_{M1}I_T & \sigma_{M2}I_T & \dots & \sigma_{MM}I_T \end{pmatrix}E(uu′)=Ω=​σ11​IT​σ21​IT​⋮σM1​IT​​σ12​IT​σ22​IT​⋮σM2​IT​​……⋱…​σ1M​IT​σ2M​IT​⋮σMM​IT​​​
  • The Identity Matrix (ITI_TIT​): This is the “inner matrix”.
    • It is a T×TT \times TT×T matrix with 1s on the diagonal and 0s elsewhere.
    • It tells the computer: “Only link these two assets if they are from the same time ttt.”
  • The Covariance (σij\sigma_{ij}σij​): This is the “scalar” multiplie. It scales the entire ITI_TIT​ block by the strength of the relationship between equation iii and equation jjj

Step 5: Derive the GLS Estimator

We look for a square matrix PPP that can “pre-whiten” the data. We define PPP such that it is the “square root” of the inverse of our master map:

Ω−1=P′P\Omega ^{-1} = P'PΩ−1=P′P

We multiply the entire stacked equation by $P$ to create a new, “clean” system:

PY⏟Y∗=PX⏟X∗β+Pu⏟u∗\underbrace{PY}_{Y^*} = \underbrace{PX}_{X^*}\beta + \underbrace{Pu}_{u^*}Y∗PY​​=X∗PX​​β+u∗Pu​​
  • The New Error (u∗u^*u∗): The covariance of this new error is now E[(Pu)(Pu)′]=PΩP′E[(Pu)(Pu)'] = P \Omega P'E[(Pu)(Pu)′]=PΩP′. Because of how we chosePPP, this simplifies to the Identity Matrix (III).

Now that the system is “clean,” we can use the standard OLS formula on it:

β^=(X∗′X∗)−1X∗′Y∗\hat{\beta} = (X^{*'}X^*)^{-1}X^{*'}Y^*β^​=(X∗′X∗)−1X∗′Y∗

Unpeel this in terms of our original Xs and Ys

β^=((PX)′(PX))−1(PX)′(PY)\hat{\beta} = ((PX)'(PX))^{-1}(PX)'(PY)β^​=((PX)′(PX))−1(PX)′(PY)

Using the matrix property (AB)′=B′A′(AB)' = B'A'(AB)′=B′A′, we expand the terms:

β^=(X′P′PX)−1X′P′PY\hat{\beta} = (X'P'PX)^{-1}X'P'PYβ^​=(X′P′PX)−1X′P′PY

Finally, recall we defined the inverse matrix as Ω−1=P′P\Omega^{-1} = P'PΩ−1=P′P:

β^SUR=(X′Ω−1X)−1  X′Ω−1Y\mathbf{\hat{\beta}_{SUR} = (X' \Omega^{-1} X)^{-1} \ \ X' \Omega^{-1} Y}β^​SUR​=(X′Ω−1X)−1  X′Ω−1Y
  • This is the SUR estimator
  • It looks just like an OLS, except we squeezed Ω−1\Omega^{-1}Ω−1 in the middel of it.

Conducting a Feasible GLS

In the real world, we do not know the true values of the σij\sigma_{ij}σij​ inside the Ω\OmegaΩ matrix. So just like a typical GLS, you actually have to esimate what that matrix is.

We must estimate them using a two-stage process:

1. First Stage (OLS): Run OLS the MMMequations separately, and retain the residuals u^it\hat{u}_{it}u^it​

2. Covariance Estimation: Calculate the estimated covariances: σ^ij=1T∑t=1Tu^itu^jt\hat{\sigma}_{ij} = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^T \hat{u}_{it} \hat{u}_{jt}σ^ij​=T1​∑t=1T​u^it​u^jt​.

3. Final Stage (FGLS): Plug these σ^ij\hat{\sigma}_{ij}σ^ij​ values back into the Ω\OmegaΩ matrix and, now we use the SUR estimator to do the GLS with this synthetic inverse system covariance matrix.

Pesaran Chapter 19

SUR Model

  • Setup

  • The error term is exogenous

  • There are three cases, the models’ errors are truly unrelated to each other (1), they are related but have the exact same regressors (2) and finally, errors they are related and the models have different regressors (3). Case 3 is what makes system estimation useful.

  • We stack the model usually

  • We find the system covariance matrix, and use that to get the estimator

  • Of course in reality, it has to be feasible

Special Cases When the error terms are actually unrelated, indeed we get nothing useful, GLS falls to OLS When the error terms are related, but the regressors are identical. Again GLS falls to OLS
