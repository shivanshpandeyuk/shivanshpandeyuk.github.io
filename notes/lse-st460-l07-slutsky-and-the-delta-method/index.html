Why we need tools that reuse one limiting result

Suppose we already have a CLT-type statement

n(Tn−θ)→dN(0,V).\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,V).n​(Tn​−θ)d​N(0,V).

In practice it is never enough on its own, because we need to:

  • replace the unknown V by an estimate;
  • transform θ into g(θ) (an odds, a log rate, a ratio);
  • combine several estimators;
  • build intervals on a scale that respects the parameter space.

Slutsky handles substitution; the delta method handles smooth transformation. Continuous mapping underpins both.

Continuous functions preserve convergence

Question. If XnX_nXn​ settles down, does g(Xn)g(X_n)g(Xn​) settle down too? Yes, as long as g has no jumps where the limit lives.

Xn→pX, g continuous at X w.p. 1 ⇒ g(Xn)→pg(X);Xn→dX, P(X∈Dg)=0 ⇒ g(Xn)→dg(X)X_n\xrightarrow{p}X,\ g\text{ continuous at }X\text{ w.p. 1}\ \Rightarrow\ g(X_n)\xrightarrow{p}g(X);\qquad X_n\xrightarrow{d}X,\ \mathbb P(X\in D_g)=0\ \Rightarrow\ g(X_n)\xrightarrow{d}g(X)Xn​p​X, g continuous at X w.p. 1 ⇒ g(Xn​)p​g(X);Xn​d​X, P(X∈Dg​)=0 ⇒ g(Xn​)d​g(X)

where DgD_gDg​ is the set of discontinuity points of g.

  • Discontinuity is allowed on a set of probability 0. That is exactly the condition: g may have jumps, provided the limiting law puts no mass on them.

Consistency version. If Tn→pθT_n\xrightarrow{p}\thetaTn​p​θ and g is continuous at θ, then g(Tn)→pg(θ)g(T_n)\xrightarrow{p}g(\theta)g(Tn​)p​g(θ).

  • This is the continuous mapping theorem: if TnT_nTn​ is a consistent estimator of θ, then g(Tn)g(T_n)g(Tn​) is a consistent estimator of g(θ).
  • Examples: Tn2→pθ2T_n^2\xrightarrow{p}\theta^2Tn2​p​θ2, eTn→peθe^{T_n}\xrightarrow{p}e^\thetaeTn​p​eθ, 1/Tn→p1/θ1/T_n\xrightarrow{p}1/\theta1/Tn​p​1/θ (θ ≠ 0).
  • Ratios. Applying continuous mapping to a ratio needs joint convergence of numerator and denominator and P(Y=0)=0\mathbb P(Y=0)=0P(Y=0)=0 for the limiting denominator; for a constant limit c this means c ≠ 0.

Why θ ≠ 0 matters for 1/T. If θ = 0, g(t) = 1/t is not continuous at the limit point: TnT_nTn​ can sit just either side of zero and 1/Tn1/T_n1/Tn​ swings between ±∞. Same reason a P/E multiple on near-zero earnings is meaningless.

Slutsky’s theorem

Question. Given Xn→dXX_n\xrightarrow{d}XXn​d​X and some other sequence YnY_nYn​ converging to a constant, can they be added, multiplied or divided?

Xn→dX,Yn→pc ⇒ Xn+Yn→dX+c,XnYn→dcX,XnYn→dXc (c≠0)X_n\xrightarrow{d}X,\quad Y_n\xrightarrow{p}c\ \Rightarrow\ X_n+Y_n\xrightarrow{d}X+c,\quad X_nY_n\xrightarrow{d}cX,\quad \frac{X_n}{Y_n}\xrightarrow{d}\frac{X}{c}\ (c\ne0)Xn​d​X,Yn​p​c ⇒ Xn​+Yn​d​X+c,Xn​Yn​d​cX,Yn​Xn​​d​cX​ (c=0)
  • Yn→pcY_n\xrightarrow{p}cYn​p​c is equivalent to Yn→dcY_n\xrightarrow{d}cYn​d​c, because the limit is a constant.
  • No independence between XnX_nXn​ and YnY_nYn​ is required: they can be dependent. The constant probability limit supplies the joint convergence for free.
  • What is essential: the limit of YnY_nYn​ is a constant. Convergence in distribution of each sequence separately does not give convergence of sums or ratios in general.

Activity 7.1: check a claim about a ratio limit

Let Z ∼ N(0, 1) and set Xn=Yn=ZX_n=Y_n=ZXn​=Yn​=Z for every n. Claim: “Slutsky’s conditions fail, so Xn/YnX_n/Y_nXn​/Yn​ cannot converge in distribution.”

  1. Direct limit. On the event Z ≠ 0, Xn/Yn=Z/Z=1X_n/Y_n=Z/Z=1Xn​/Yn​=Z/Z=1 for every n. Since P(Z=0)=0\mathbb P(Z=0)=0P(Z=0)=0, Xn/Yn=1X_n/Y_n=1Xn​/Yn​=1 with probability one, so Xn/Yn→d1X_n/Y_n\xrightarrow{d}1Xn​/Yn​d​1 (point mass at 1). The “with probability one” is needed because the ratio is a random variable.
  2. Which condition fails. The ratio rule needs Yn→pcY_n\xrightarrow{p}cYn​p​c with c a non-zero constant. Here Yn=ZY_n=ZYn​=Z is a random variable for every n, not converging to a constant, so Slutsky’s ratio rule does not apply.
  3. Verdict. The claim is false. Slutsky’s conditions are sufficient, not necessary: failing them means the theorem is silent, not that convergence fails.

Studentisation and the estimated asymptotic SE

Studentisation. Suppose n(Tn−θ)→dN(0,σ2)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)n​(Tn​−θ)d​N(0,σ2) and σ^n→pσ>0\hat\sigma_n\xrightarrow{p}\sigma>0σ^n​p​σ>0.

  1. Set Xn=n(Tn−θ)X_n=\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)Xn​=n​(Tn​−θ), which converges in distribution to N(0, σ²), a distribution.
  2. Set Yn=σ^nY_n=\hat\sigma_nYn​=σ^n​, which converges in probability to σ > 0, a non-zero constant.
  3. Slutsky’s ratio rule:
XnYn=n(Tn−θ)σ^n→d1σN(0,σ2)=N(0,1).\frac{X_n}{Y_n}=\frac{\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)}{\hat\sigma_n}\xrightarrow{d}\frac{1}{\sigma}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)=\mathcal N(0,1).Yn​Xn​​=σ^n​n​(Tn​−θ)​d​σ1​N(0,σ2)=N(0,1).
  • This is how the Slutsky result is used: the limit is the standard normal. Here σ is the SD of the limiting law of n(Tn−θ)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)n​(Tn​−θ), and the estimated SE of TnT_nTn​ is σ^n/n\hat\sigma_n/\sqrt nσ^n​/n​.
  • This is exactly the step L6 deferred: n(Xˉn−μ)/Sn→dN(0,1)\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/S_n\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1)n​(Xˉn​−μ)/Sn​d​N(0,1) under CLT conditions, without normal data.

Estimated asymptotic SE (self-study recap). If n(Tn−θ)→dN(0,V)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,V)n​(Tn​−θ)d​N(0,V), V > 0, then “for large n”

Tn ∼˙ N(θ,Vn),SE^(Tn)=V^nn,V^n≥0, V^n→pV.T_n\ \dot\sim\ \mathcal N\Big(\theta,\frac Vn\Big),\qquad \widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n)=\sqrt{\frac{\hat V_n}{n}},\quad \hat V_n\ge0,\ \hat V_n\xrightarrow{p}V.Tn​ ∼˙ N(θ,nV​),SE(Tn​)=nV^n​​​,V^n​≥0, V^n​p​V.
  • The studentised version again follows from Slutsky’s ratio result.
  • V is the variance of the limiting normal law. Concluding n Var(Tn)→Vn\,\mathrm{Var}(T_n)\to VnVar(Tn​)→V needs extra assumptions that control extreme values of n(Tn−θ)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)n​(Tn​−θ), roughly “no fat tails”. Convergence in distribution does not imply convergence of moments (uniform integrability is the technical condition, not examinable here).

Moments can diverge while the law converges. Let Wn=n(Tn−θ)W_n=\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)Wn​=n​(Tn​−θ) equal a N(0, 1) draw with probability 1 − 1/n and the value n with probability 1/n. Then Wn→dN(0,1)W_n\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1)Wn​d​N(0,1), but Var(Wn)≈n2⋅1n=n→∞\mathrm{Var}(W_n)\approx n^2\cdot\frac1n=n\to\inftyVar(Wn​)≈n2⋅n1​=n→∞. The asymptotic variance is 1; the actual variance explodes.

Wald confidence intervals

If, by Slutsky, (Tn−θ)/SE^(Tn)→dN(0,1)(T_n-\theta)/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1)(Tn​−θ)/SE(Tn​)d​N(0,1), then for 0 < α < 1 an approximate 100(1 − α)% interval is

Tn±z1−α/2 SE^(Tn),T_n\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n),Tn​±z1−α/2​SE(Tn​),

with zqz_qzq​ the q quantile of N(0, 1).

Derivation.

  1. P(−z1−α/2≤(Tn−θ)/SE^≤z1−α/2)→1−α\mathbb P\big(-z_{1-\alpha/2}\le(T_n-\theta)/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\le z_{1-\alpha/2}\big)\to1-\alphaP(−z1−α/2​≤(Tn​−θ)/SE≤z1−α/2​)→1−α by the limit above and continuity of Φ.
  2. Rearrange the event for θ: Tn−z1−α/2SE^≤θ≤Tn+z1−α/2SE^T_n-z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\le\theta\le T_n+z_{1-\alpha/2}\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}Tn​−z1−α/2​SE≤θ≤Tn​+z1−α/2​SE.
  • Interpret carefully: the interval is random, θ is fixed. As n → ∞ the probability that the random interval covers θ tends to 1 − α; for large n about 100(1 − α)% of intervals from repeated samples cover θ.
  • This is how frequentists define confidence intervals: a statement about the procedure over repeated samples, not a probability that θ lies in one realised interval.

The univariate delta method

Question. Given how TnT_nTn​ fluctuates around θ, how does g(Tn)g(T_n)g(Tn​) fluctuate around g(θ)? Near θ a smooth g is a straight line with slope g′(θ), so errors get multiplied by g′(θ).

Result. For a non-degenerate first-order limit, suppose n(Tn−θ)→dN(0,V)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,V)n​(Tn​−θ)d​N(0,V) with V > 0, and let g be differentiable at θ with g′(θ)≠0g'(\theta)\ne0g′(θ)=0. Then

n{g(Tn)−g(θ)}→dN(0,[g′(θ)]2V).\sqrt n\{g(T_n)-g(\theta)\}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N\big(0,[g'(\theta)]^2V\big).n​{g(Tn​)−g(θ)}d​N(0,[g′(θ)]2V).

This is the key result of the lecture, and the conditions matter.

Derivation.

  1. Stochastic Taylor expansion (differentiability at θ):
g(Tn)−g(θ)=g′(θ)(Tn−θ)+rn,rn=op(∣Tn−θ∣).g(T_n)-g(\theta)=g'(\theta)(T_n-\theta)+r_n,\qquad r_n=o_p(|T_n-\theta|).g(Tn​)−g(θ)=g′(θ)(Tn​−θ)+rn​,rn​=op​(∣Tn​−θ∣).
  1. Multiply by n\sqrt nn​:
n{g(Tn)−g(θ)}=g′(θ)n(Tn−θ)+n rn.\sqrt n\{g(T_n)-g(\theta)\}=g'(\theta)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)+\sqrt n\,r_n.n​{g(Tn​)−g(θ)}=g′(θ)n​(Tn​−θ)+n​rn​.
  1. Remainder vanishes. n(Tn−θ)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)n​(Tn​−θ) is bounded in probability (it converges in distribution), and rn/∣Tn−θ∣→p0r_n/|T_n-\theta|\xrightarrow{p}0rn​/∣Tn​−θ∣p​0, so n rn→p0\sqrt n\,r_n\xrightarrow{p}0n​rn​p​0.
  2. Leading term. g′(θ)n(Tn−θ)→dg′(θ) N(0,V)=N(0,[g′(θ)]2V)g'(\theta)\sqrt n(T_n-\theta)\xrightarrow{d}g'(\theta)\,\mathcal N(0,V)=\mathcal N(0,[g'(\theta)]^2V)g′(θ)n​(Tn​−θ)d​g′(θ)N(0,V)=N(0,[g′(θ)]2V) (continuous mapping, multiplication by a constant).
  3. Slutsky (sum with a term converging to the constant 0) gives the stated limit.
  • Interpretation: locally, g multiplies estimation errors by g′(θ) and the asymptotic variance by [g′(θ)]2[g'(\theta)]^2[g′(θ)]2.
  • Why g′(θ) ≠ 0: if it is zero, the limit is N(0, 0), a point mass; the first-order term carries no information and the right scale changes (see the zero-derivative section below).

The transformed standard error

Under the delta method conditions, also suppose g′ is continuous at θ. Define

SE^{g(Tn)}:=∣g′(Tn)∣ SE^(Tn).\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\{g(T_n)\}:=|g'(T_n)|\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n).SE{g(Tn​)}:=∣g′(Tn​)∣SE(Tn​).
  • Since Tn→pθT_n\xrightarrow{p}\thetaTn​p​θ, continuous mapping gives g′(Tn)→pg′(θ)g'(T_n)\xrightarrow{p}g'(\theta)g′(Tn​)p​g′(θ), so Slutsky allows plugging in the estimated derivative.
  • The absolute value keeps the SE non-negative when g is decreasing.
  • The non-zero g′(θ) condition is visible here. For large n this is a good approximation to the SE of the random variable g(Tn)g(T_n)g(Tn​).

Activity 7.2: estimate a reciprocal and its standard error

A positive estimator has observed value Tn=2.00T_n=2.00Tn​=2.00 and estimated asymptotic SE 0.10. The estimand is φ = 1/θ with θ > 0. Plug-in estimate and delta-method SE: 0.025, 0.05 or 0.20?

  1. Function. g(t) = 1/t, so g′(t)=−t−2g'(t)=-t^{-2}g′(t)=−t−2.
  2. Plug-in estimate. ϕ^=g(Tn)=1/2.00=0.50\hat\phi=g(T_n)=1/2.00=0.50ϕ^​=g(Tn​)=1/2.00=0.50.
  3. SE. SE^(ϕ^)=∣g′(Tn)∣ SE^(Tn)=∣−2−2∣×0.10=0.25×0.10=0.025\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\hat\phi)=|g'(T_n)|\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n)=|-2^{-2}|\times0.10=0.25\times0.10=0.025SE(ϕ^​)=∣g′(Tn​)∣SE(Tn​)=∣−2−2∣×0.10=0.25×0.10=0.025.

Note that g is the function g(t) = 1/t, and g(Tn)=1/Tng(T_n)=1/T_ng(Tn​)=1/Tn​ is its value at the estimator. Keeping these apart matters when differentiating: differentiate g in t, then evaluate at TnT_nTn​. The answer also needs the plug-in estimate ϕ^=0.5\hat\phi=0.5ϕ^​=0.5, not only the SE.

Log transformation gives multiplicative uncertainty

Question. θ > 0 (a rate, a variance, a price). A Wald interval Tn±zSE^T_n\pm z\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}Tn​±zSE can dip below zero. Fix: build the interval for log θ, then exponentiate.

  1. g(t) = log t, g′(θ) = 1/θ, so n{log⁡Tn−log⁡θ}→dN(0,V/θ2)\sqrt n\{\log T_n-\log\theta\}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,V/\theta^2)n​{logTn​−logθ}d​N(0,V/θ2).
  2. Transformed SE: SE^(log⁡Tn)=dlog⁡tdt∣TnSE^(Tn)=SE^(Tn)/Tn\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(\log T_n)=\dfrac{d\log t}{dt}\Big|_{T_n}\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n)=\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n)/T_nSE(logTn​)=dtdlogt​​Tn​​SE(Tn​)=SE(Tn​)/Tn​.
  3. Wald interval for log θ: log⁡Tn±z1−α/2 SE^(Tn)/Tn\log T_n\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n)/T_nlogTn​±z1−α/2​SE(Tn​)/Tn​.
  4. Exponentiate the endpoints (exp is increasing):
Tnexp⁡{±z1−α/2SE^(Tn)Tn}.T_n\exp\Big\{\pm z_{1-\alpha/2}\frac{\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}(T_n)}{T_n}\Big\}.Tn​exp{±z1−α/2​Tn​SE(Tn​)​}.
  • The endpoints divide and multiply TnT_nTn​ by the same factor: asymmetric on the original scale, always positive.
  • The point is the range restriction: a plain Wald CI can breach it, so map to logs. The resulting interval can be very asymmetric.

Worked number. Tn=2.0T_n=2.0Tn​=2.0, SE^=0.5\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}=0.5SE=0.5, 95%. Plain Wald: 2.0 ± 1.96(0.5) = (1.02, 2.98). Log scale: factor e1.96×0.25=e0.49=1.632e^{1.96\times0.25}=e^{0.49}=1.632e1.96×0.25=e0.49=1.632, interval (2.0/1.632, 2.0×1.632) = (1.23, 3.26). With SE = 1.2 the plain Wald lower end is 2.0 − 2.35 < 0, impossible for a rate; the log interval stays positive. Same logic as quoting a return interval in log returns.

Logit intervals stay between zero and one

Watch for two over-generalisations. First, the sign of the covariance effect depends on g, not only on the covariance: it is the sign of g1g2V12g_1g_2V_{12}g1​g2​V12​. Finance analogue. A P/E built from price and earnings estimates that are positively correlated is less noisy than the two inputs suggest; an EV built by adding positively correlated components is noisier. Same formula, opposite sign of g1g2g_1g_2g1​g2​.

Summary

  • Continuous mapping preserves convergence under suitable continuity conditions.
  • Slutsky justifies substituting consistent estimators of unknown constants.
  • The delta method uses derivatives and covariance to approximate transformed uncertainty.
  • On the chosen scale, Wald intervals are estimate ± normal quantile × estimated asymptotic SE.
  • Zero derivatives, non-differentiable transformations and boundaries need care.
  • Next (L8): specify the estimand, model and assumptions these tools serve.

Traps

  • Applying Slutsky when YnY_nYn​ converges to a random variable (Activity 7.1), or concluding “no convergence” because Slutsky does not apply.
  • Forgetting c ≠ 0 in the ratio rule, or θ ≠ 0 for 1/T.
  • Treating asymptotic variance V as the limit of n Var(Tn)n\,\mathrm{Var}(T_n)nVar(Tn​) without extra conditions.
  • Dropping the absolute value in ∣g′(Tn)∣SE^|g'(T_n)|\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}∣g′(Tn​)∣SE, or writing g = 1/TnT_nTn​ instead of g(t) = 1/t.
  • Using the delta method when g′(θ) = 0 and reporting N(0, 0); switch to scale n (χ² limit).
  • Using the delta method at a kink (|t| at 0); only continuous mapping applies.
  • Ignoring the covariance term, or assuming positive covariance always reduces variance (it depends on the signs of the partials).
  • Plain Wald intervals for positive or bounded parameters that cross the boundary.
  • Reading a 95% CI as “95% probability θ is in this interval”.
