What is optimisation?

Given a real-valued function of d variables, where is it smallest (or largest), possibly with restrictions on where to look? Optimisation is the mathematical discipline concerned with finding maxima and minima of functions, possibly subject to constraints.

General form.

min⁡x (or max⁡) f(x1,…,xd)subject togi(x1,…,xd)≥0 or =0,(x1,…,xd)∈S.\min_{x}\ (\text{or }\max)\ f(x_1,\dots,x_d)\quad\text{subject to}\quad g_i(x_1,\dots,x_d)\ge0\text{ or }=0,\quad (x_1,\dots,x_d)\in S.xmin​ (or max) f(x1​,…,xd​)subject togi​(x1​,…,xd​)≥0 or =0,(x1​,…,xd​)∈S.
  • f is the objective function; the gig_igi​ are functional constraints; S gives set constraints; x1,…,xdx_1,\dots,x_dx1​,…,xd​ are the decision variables.
  • Only minimisation is treated: maximising f is the same as minimising −f.
  • An analytical optimum is often hard to find for an arbitrary f, so the course approaches the problem numerically.

Types of models. Stochastic (probabilistic information on the data) vs deterministic (data certain). Deterministic splits into discrete/integer (S=ZdS=\mathbb Z^dS=Zd) and continuous (S=RdS=\mathbb R^dS=Rd); each can be convex (f and the feasible set convex) or non-convex; convex problems split into linear (f and the g’s linear) and nonlinear.

Discrete vs continuous

Discrete optimisation combines combinatorics, graph theory and linear programming to optimise over discrete structures; the travelling salesman problem (Lecture 3) is the standard example.

  • Discrete problems are typically harder because of the combinatorial explosion: two variables with 10 values each give 10 × 10 = 100 candidates; ten such variables give 101010^{10}1010.
  • The rest of the course focuses on continuous optimisation.

Constrained vs unconstrained

Unconstrained: min f(x1,…,xd)f(x_1,\dots,x_d)f(x1​,…,xd​). Constrained: the same with restrictions. One objective, f(x,y)=x2+2y2f(x,y)=x^2+2y^2f(x,y)=x2+2y2, under four regimes:

  1. Unconstrained. f≥0f\ge0f≥0 with equality only at (0, 0): minimum 0 at the origin.
  2. x > 0. f can be made as close to 0 as desired (take y = 0, x → 0⁺) but x = 0 is excluded: the infimum 0 is not attained, so there is no minimiser. Strict inequalities give open feasible sets; this is why constraints are normally written with ≥.
  3. −2 < x < 5, y ≥ 1. Minimise each term separately: x2x^2x2 is smallest at x = 0 (allowed), 2y22y^22y2 at the boundary y = 1. Minimum 2 at (0, 1): the constraint is active in y only.
  4. x + y = 2 (Lagrange). L=x2+2y2+λ(x+y−2)L=x^2+2y^2+\lambda(x+y-2)L=x2+2y2+λ(x+y−2).
    1. ∂L/∂x=2x+λ=0\partial L/\partial x=2x+\lambda=0∂L/∂x=2x+λ=0 and ∂L/∂y=4y+λ=0\partial L/\partial y=4y+\lambda=0∂L/∂y=4y+λ=0, so 2x=4y2x=4y2x=4y, i.e. x = 2y.
    2. Substitute into x + y = 2: 3y = 2, so y = 2/3, x = 4/3.
    3. Value 16/9+8/9=24/9=8/316/9+8/9=24/9=8/316/9+8/9=24/9=8/3. Check by substitution: (2−y)2+2y2(2-y)^2+2y^2(2−y)2+2y2 has derivative −2(2−y)+4y=6y−4=0-2(2-y)+4y=6y-4=0−2(2−y)+4y=6y−4=0 at y = 2/3.

The constraint can exclude the region where the unconstrained optimum lives, so the constrained optimum sits on the boundary of the feasible set.

Why constrained is harder.

  • With equality constraints only, the method of Lagrange multipliers converts the problem into finding stationary points of an unconstrained function with (original variables + number of equality constraints) variables. In 2D with one constraint: L(x1,x2,λ)=f(x1,x2)+λg(x1,x2)L(x_1,x_2,\lambda)=f(x_1,x_2)+\lambda g(x_1,x_2)L(x1​,x2​,λ)=f(x1​,x2​)+λg(x1​,x2​).
  • If the objective and all hard constraints are linear, it is a linear programming problem, solved by the simplex method: fast in practice but exponential in the worst case (Klee-Minty, 1972). Interior-point methods solve LPs in polynomial time.

Local minimum, global minimum, stationary point

  • A global minimum is the smallest value of f over the given set. A local minimum is a minimum within some neighbourhood.
  • Every global minimum is a local minimum; a local minimum need not be global.
  • A stationary (critical) point is where all partial derivatives vanish, i.e. the gradient is zero. One equation per input variable: a function of two inputs (a surface in 3D) needs both ∂f/∂x=0\partial f/\partial x=0∂f/∂x=0 and ∂f/∂y=0\partial f/\partial y=0∂f/∂y=0.
  • In 1D a stationary point is a local max, a local min or an inflection point. So f′(x)=0f'(x)=0f′(x)=0 is necessary for an interior minimum of a differentiable f, not sufficient.

Convex vs non-convex

Intuition. Pick any two points on the graph and join them with a straight chord. f is convex if the graph never rises above that chord.

Definition. f:Rd→Rf:\mathbb R^d\to\mathbb Rf:Rd→R is convex if for all x, y and all t∈[0,1]t\in[0,1]t∈[0,1]

t f(x)+(1−t) f(y) ≥ f(tx+(1−t)y).t\,f(x)+(1-t)\,f(y)\ \ge\ f\big(tx+(1-t)y\big).tf(x)+(1−t)f(y) ≥ f(tx+(1−t)y).
  • Left side: the point a fraction t along the chord between (x,f(x))(x,f(x))(x,f(x)) and (y,f(y))(y,f(y))(y,f(y)). Right side: the function at the matching input. In 1D with end points x1,x2x_1,x_2x1​,x2​ (any two points, not necessarily symmetric about anything), the chord point is tf(x1)+(1−t)f(x2)tf(x_1)+(1-t)f(x_2)tf(x1​)+(1−t)f(x2​); watch the brackets.
  • In 2D, the surface value at the mixed input sits below the chord between the two end points.

Convexity, continuity and differentiability.

  • Not always differentiable: f(x)=∣x∣f(x)=|x|f(x)=∣x∣ is convex (the chord inequality is the triangle inequality) but has no derivative at 0. Similarly max⁡(x,0)\max(x,0)max(x,0).
  • Continuous on an open interval: a convex f on (a, b) is continuous there; at interior points it even has left and right derivatives. On a closed interval it can jump at the end points: f = 1 at x = 0 and f = 0 on (0, 1] is convex on [0, 1] but not continuous at 0.
  • Continuous does not imply convex: sin x is continuous and not convex.

Convex functions are common.

  • Least squares ∥y−Xβ∥2\lVert y-X\beta\rVert^2∥y−Xβ∥2 is convex in β (its Hessian 2X⊤X2X^\top X2X⊤X is positive semidefinite).
  • In 1D, a smooth f with f′′(x)≥0f''(x)\ge0f′′(x)≥0 for all x is convex. The “for all x” matters: f′′≥0f''\ge0f′′≥0 at one point says nothing.
  • Many non-convex functions are locally convex, for example in the wells around a local and a global optimum, so convex-problem techniques can still be used near a minimum.

Theorem: any local minimum of a convex function is a global minimum

Setting. f convex on a convex set, x∗x^*x∗ a local minimum: there is r > 0 with f(z)≥f(x∗)f(z)\ge f(x^*)f(z)≥f(x∗) whenever ∥z−x∗∥<r\lVert z-x^*\rVert<r∥z−x∗∥<r.

  1. Suppose not. Then some y has f(y)<f(x∗)f(y)<f(x^*)f(y)<f(x∗).
  2. Walk from x∗x^*x∗ towards y. For t∈(0,1]t\in(0,1]t∈(0,1] let zt=(1−t)x∗+tyz_t=(1-t)x^*+tyzt​=(1−t)x∗+ty. Its distance from x∗x^*x∗ is t∥y−x∗∥t\lVert y-x^*\rVertt∥y−x∗∥, which is less than r once t is small enough.
  3. Apply convexity: f(zt)≤(1−t)f(x∗)+tf(y)f(z_t)\le(1-t)f(x^*)+tf(y)f(zt​)≤(1−t)f(x∗)+tf(y).
  4. Use step 1: since f(y)<f(x∗)f(y)<f(x^*)f(y)<f(x∗) and t > 0, the right side is strictly less than (1−t)f(x∗)+tf(x∗)=f(x∗)(1-t)f(x^*)+tf(x^*)=f(x^*)(1−t)f(x∗)+tf(x∗)=f(x∗).
  5. So points ztz_tzt​ arbitrarily close to x∗x^*x∗ have f(zt)<f(x∗)f(z_t)<f(x^*)f(zt​)<f(x∗), contradicting local minimality. Hence no such y exists and x∗x^*x∗ is global. ∎

Contradiction is natural here because “local” is a statement about a neighbourhood and the aim is to rule out one bad point anywhere. But “for any” does not demand contradiction in general: many universal statements are proved directly (fix an arbitrary x and argue). Pick the method by structure, not by the word.

Corollary. If f is convex and differentiable on ℝ, then f′(x∗)=0f'(x^*)=0f′(x∗)=0 ⇒ x∗x^*x∗ is a global minimiser. Reason: convexity gives f(y)≥f(x∗)+f′(x∗)(y−x∗)f(y)\ge f(x^*)+f'(x^*)(y-x^*)f(y)≥f(x∗)+f′(x∗)(y−x∗) for all y (the tangent lies below the graph), and the last term is 0.

The role of optimisation in statistics

A real-world problem, plus reasonable assumptions, abstraction, data and simplifications, gives a statistical model with a few parameters: this is the objective function. An optimisation algorithm produces the fitted model; interpretation gives a solution; then ask whether it makes sense and, if not, change the model or assumptions.

Precisely: the model defines the objective, e.g. the negative log-likelihood −ℓ(θ)-\ell(\theta)−ℓ(θ) or a residual sum of squares; the parameters are the decision variables. MLE, least squares, ridge, lasso and logistic regression are all instances.

Choosing a method; the problem in focus

The method depends on: the size d; whether ∇f\nabla f∇f and the Hessian ∇2f\nabla^2f∇2f are available; the accuracy required; global or local minimum; and the convexity of f.

The focus is the unconstrained smooth (convex) problem min⁡x∈Rdf(x)\min_{x\in\mathbb R^d}f(x)minx∈Rd​f(x), starting with d = 1.

One-dimensional optimisation: bisection

Idea. If f is differentiable and x minimises it, then f′(x)=0f'(x)=0f′(x)=0. For convex f the converse holds too, so minimising f is equivalent to finding a root of f′. Aim: find x with ∣x−x∗∣<ε|x-x^*|<\varepsilon∣x−x∗∣<ε, or a sequence xkx^kxk with ∣xk−x∗∣<ε|x^k-x^*|<\varepsilon∣xk−x∗∣<ε for large k.

Bracket. Suppose f′ can be evaluated anywhere and there are a < b with f′(a)<0f'(a)<0f′(a)<0 and f′(b)>0f'(b)>0f′(b)>0, f′ continuous. By the intermediate value theorem f′ has a zero in (a, b).

Algorithm.

  1. If b − a is sufficiently small, return a (or b, or anything between) and stop. This is the base case, since a ≈ b.
  2. Else compute the midpoint c = (a + b)/2.
  3. Evaluate f′(c).
  4. Replace either (a, f′(a)) or (b, f′(b)) by (c, f′(c)) so that a zero crossing stays inside the new interval: if f′(c) < 0 set a = c, else set b = c.

The method needs opposite signs at the two ends: f′(a1)<0f'(a_1)<0f′(a1​)<0 on the left and f′(b1)>0f'(b_1)>0f′(b1​)>0 on the right. The brackets [ak,bk][a_k,b_k][ak​,bk​] are nested, with midpoints c2,c3,…c_2,c_3,\dotsc2​,c3​,….

Newton’s method

Bisection uses only the sign of the first derivative. If f″ is also known, Newton’s method (Newton-Raphson) does better.

Derivation.

  1. Linearise f′ at the current point (first-order Taylor): f′(xk+1)≈f′(xk)+f′′(xk)(xk+1−xk)f'(x^{k+1})\approx f'(x^k)+f''(x^k)(x^{k+1}-x^k)f′(xk+1)≈f′(xk)+f′′(xk)(xk+1−xk).

  3. Solve for xk+1x^{k+1}xk+1 (needs f′′(xk)≠0f''(x^k)\ne0f′′(xk)=0):

History. Raphson published the method in Analysis Aequationum Universalis (1690); Newton’s similar formula in the Method of Fluxions appeared only in 1736. The modern calculus form x − f(x)/f′(x) is due to Thomas Simpson (1740), who also noted its use for optimisation by setting the derivative to zero. Stigler’s law: no scientific discovery is named after its original discoverer (Stigler credited it to Merton, so the law obeys itself).

Convergence criteria

Template: x0x^0x0 = initial guess; k = 0; while (???): xk+1=xk−f′(xk)/f′′(xk)x^{k+1}=x^k-f'(x^k)/f''(x^k)xk+1=xk−f′(xk)/f′′(xk), k = k + 1. What goes in the while? Candidates: ∣xk−xk−1∣<ε|x^k-x^{k-1}|<\varepsilon∣xk−xk−1∣<ε and ∣f′(xk)∣<ε|f'(x^k)|<\varepsilon∣f′(xk)∣<ε.

  • Also cap the iterations: stop at k=kmax⁡k=k_{\max}k=kmax​ and report failure.

Why both checks.

  • A Δx check is needed. If f′ is very flat, ∣f′(xk)∣<ε|f'(x^k)|<\varepsilon∣f′(xk)∣<ε although x∗x^*x∗ is far away. The step ∣xk−xk−1∣|x^k-x^{k-1}|∣xk−xk−1∣ is still large, which reveals that the iterates have not settled.
  • An f′(x) check is needed. If f′ is very steep, the step is tiny although f′(xk)f'(x^k)f′(xk) is still far from zero.
  • Each check alone can be fooled by the shape of f′; together they guard against both failure modes.

When Newton’s method fails

Example one: bad starting point. On a wiggly f′, the tangent from a poor start can send the iterates back and forth between two points (oscillation) or out along a flat tail where f′ → 0 but never reaches zero (converges to ∞).

  • Remedies: a hybrid, with bisection for the first few iterations and Newton afterwards; or try multiple starting points.

Example two: divergence. f(x)=34x4/3f(x)=\frac34x^{4/3}f(x)=43​x4/3, stationary point x∗=0x^*=0x∗=0.

  1. f′(x)=x1/3f'(x)=x^{1/3}f′(x)=x1/3, f′′(x)=13x−2/3f''(x)=\frac13x^{-2/3}f′′(x)=31​x−2/3.
  2. Newton step: xk+1=xk−(xk)1/313(xk)−2/3=xk−3xk=−2xkx^{k+1}=x^k-\frac{(x^k)^{1/3}}{\frac13(x^k)^{-2/3}}=x^k-3x^k=-2x^kxk+1=xk−31​(xk)−2/3(xk)1/3​=xk−3xk=−2xk.
  3. From x0=1x^0=1x0=1: −2, 4, −8, …; ∣xk∣=2k∣x0∣|x^k|=2^k|x^0|∣xk∣=2k∣x0∣, sign alternating.

From any x0≠0x^0\ne0x0=0, however close to 0, the iterates move away: the error doubles each step. The cause is f″: it blows up as x → 0 (f′′(x)=13∣x∣−2/3f''(x)=\frac13|x|^{-2/3}f′′(x)=31​∣x∣−2/3), so f′ is steep at the root but flat away from it, and the tangent from xkx^kxk overshoots to −2xk-2x^k−2xk. Robust 1D methods in practice

  • Golden-section search: like bisection but on f itself, no derivatives; the bracket shrinks by a factor 0.618 per step ((5−1)/2=0.6180(\sqrt5-1)/2=0.6180(5​−1)/2=0.6180). R’s optimize() combines it with parabolic interpolation (Brent’s method).
  • Brent’s root finder: keeps a bracket like bisection but takes secant or inverse quadratic interpolation steps when they behave; always converges and is usually fast. The “bad starting point” hybrid done carefully. R uniroot(); Python scipy.optimize.brentq.
  • In statistics: Newton-Raphson for maximum likelihood takes f = −log-likelihood, so f″ is the observed information. Fisher scoring uses the expected information instead; for GLMs with canonical link (e.g. logistic regression) the two coincide, and glm() fits by IRLS, i.e. Newton’s method.
  • Examples: minimize_scalar(lambda x: (x - 2)**2).x returns 1.9999999999999998 (Brent); brentq(lambda x: x**3 - 2, 0, 2) returns 1.2599210498949451, and 21/3=1.259921049894872^{1/3}=1.2599210498948721/3=1.25992104989487.

Finance analogue. Solving for a bond’s yield to maturity or an option’s implied volatility is 1D root finding. Newton on implied vol uses vega as the derivative and is fast near the answer, but vega is tiny deep in or out of the money, which is condition 1 failing; production code brackets first (Brent) for exactly that reason.

Gentle §6.1, pp. 251-257: secant and regula falsi. The secant method replaces the derivative in Newton’s step by the slope through the last two iterates (Algorithm 6.4); like Newton, the two points need not bracket the root, so it can fail from a poor start. Regula falsi (Algorithm 6.5, the “Illinois” modification) keeps a bracket as in bisection but places the new point where the secant crosses zero; it generally converges more slowly than the secant method. Brent’s method combines these ideas. Gentle’s Figures 6.3 and 6.4 (pp. 252-253) show two Newton failures caused by a starting point too far from the zero. Gentle also calls the finite-difference version of Newton (eq. 6.9) the “discrete Newton’s method”, essentially the secant method.
