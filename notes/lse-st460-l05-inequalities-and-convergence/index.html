Why we need asymptotics

The big question: how can a random statistic become predictably close to a target?

An estimator is not one random variable but a sequence T1,T2,…T_1,T_2,\dotsT1​,T2​,…, one for each sample size, because its sampling distribution changes with n. The aim is to make precise claims of the form

Tn is close to θ with high probability when n is large.T_n\ \text{is close to}\ \theta\ \text{with high probability when } n \text{ is large}.Tn​ is close to θ with high probability when n is large.

Convergence is the language for how a sequence of random variables behaves as n grows. Lecture 5 builds the tools (inequalities, three modes of convergence); Lecture 6 applies them to sample means (LLN and CLT).

Markov’s inequality: a mean controls a tail

Intuition. If a non-negative quantity has a small average, it cannot be large very often: too much mass far out would drag the mean up.

X≥0,  E[X]<∞,  a>0 ⟹ P(X≥a)≤E[X]aX\ge0,\ \ \mathbb E[X]<\infty,\ \ a>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbb P(X\ge a)\le\frac{\mathbb E[X]}{a}X≥0,  E[X]<∞,  a>0 ⟹ P(X≥a)≤aE[X]​

Proof.

  1. Define the step function g(x)=a 1{x≥a}g(x)=a\,\mathbf 1\{x\ge a\}g(x)=a1{x≥a}: it is 0 for 0≤x<a0\le x<a0≤x<a and equals a for x≥ax\ge ax≥a.

  2. Pointwise comparison: for every value of a non-negative X,

    X≥a 1{X≥a}X\ge a\,\mathbf 1\{X\ge a\}X≥a1{X≥a}

    If X<aX<aX<a the right side is 0 and X≥0X\ge0X≥0; if X≥aX\ge aX≥a the right side is a and X≥aX\ge aX≥a. In a picture, the step function sits under the line y = x.

  3. Take expectations (expectation preserves ≤):

    E[a 1{X≥a}]≤E[X]\mathbb E\big[a\,\mathbf 1\{X\ge a\}\big]\le\mathbb E[X]E[a1{X≥a}]≤E[X]

  4. The expectation of an indicator is a probability, and a comes out as a constant:

    a P(X≥a)≤E[X] ⟹ P(X≥a)≤1a E[X]a\,\mathbb P(X\ge a)\le\mathbb E[X]\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbb P(X\ge a)\le\tfrac1a\,\mathbb E[X]aP(X≥a)≤E[X] ⟹ P(X≥a)≤a1​E[X]

This only holds for non-negative random variables. For a general Y, apply Markov to a non-negative transform such as ∣Y∣|Y|∣Y∣ or (Y−μ)2(Y-\mu)^2(Y−μ)2.

Note that fXf_XfX​ is reserved for the density of X, so the step function gets another name (ggg), and the indicator is of the event {x≥a}\{x\ge a\}{x≥a}, not {x=a}\{x=a\}{x=a}.

Worked example (slide). Non-negative loss L with E[L]=2\mathbb E[L]=2E[L]=2:

P(L≥10)≤210=0.20\mathbb P(L\ge10)\le\frac{2}{10}=0.20P(L≥10)≤102​=0.20
  • The bound is attained when L = 10 with probability 0.2 and L = 0 otherwise (mean 2 exactly). So Markov cannot be improved without more information.
  • The bound holds for every non-negative distribution with this mean; for a specific distribution the true tail may be far smaller.

Chebyshev’s inequality: a variance controls deviations

E[X]=μ,  Var(X)=σ2<∞,  ε>0 ⟹ P(∣X−μ∣≥ε)≤σ2ε2\mathbb E[X]=\mu,\ \ \mathrm{Var}(X)=\sigma^2<\infty,\ \ \varepsilon>0\ \Longrightarrow\ \mathbb P(|X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}E[X]=μ,  Var(X)=σ2<∞,  ε>0 ⟹ P(∣X−μ∣≥ε)≤ε2σ2​

Proof.

  1. Square both sides of the event (both sides non-negative, so nothing changes):

    P(∣X−μ∣≥ε)=P((X−μ)2≥ε2)\mathbb P(|X-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)=\mathbb P\big((X-\mu)^2\ge\varepsilon^2\big)P(∣X−μ∣≥ε)=P((X−μ)2≥ε2)

  2. (X−μ)2(X-\mu)^2(X−μ)2 is non-negative with mean σ2\sigma^2σ2, so apply Markov with a=ε2a=\varepsilon^2a=ε2:

    P((X−μ)2≥ε2)≤E(X−μ)2ε2=σ2ε2\mathbb P\big((X-\mu)^2\ge\varepsilon^2\big)\le\frac{\mathbb E(X-\mu)^2}{\varepsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{\varepsilon^2}P((X−μ)2≥ε2)≤ε2E(X−μ)2​=ε2σ2​

An alternative route first divides through by σ², so the variable (X−μ)2/σ2(X-\mu)^2/\sigma^2(X−μ)2/σ2 has expectation 1, then uses Markov with a=ε2/σ2a=\varepsilon^2/\sigma^2a=ε2/σ2. Same answer, one extra step.

The k-sigma form. Put ε=kσ\varepsilon=k\sigmaε=kσ and take complements:

P(∣X−μ∣<kσ)≥1−1k2\mathbb P(|X-\mu|<k\sigma)\ge1-\frac1{k^2}P(∣X−μ∣<kσ)≥1−k21​
  • In a picture: the area under fXf_XfX​ between μ−kσ\mu-k\sigmaμ−kσ and μ+kσ\mu+k\sigmaμ+kσ is at least 1−1/k21-1/k^21−1/k2, whatever the shape of the density.
  • Numbers: k = 2 gives at least 75% within two SDs; k = 3 gives at least 88.9%. For a normal the true figures are 95.4% and 99.7%.

Watch the inequality directions. Markov bounds the upper tail, so the event inside must be ≥\ge≥: P((X−μ)2/σ2≥ε2/σ2)≤σ2/ε2\mathbb P\big((X-\mu)^2/\sigma^2\ge\varepsilon^2/\sigma^2\big)\le\sigma^2/\varepsilon^2P((X−μ)2/σ2≥ε2/σ2)≤σ2/ε2. With ε=kσ\varepsilon=k\sigmaε=kσ the threshold is ε2/σ2=k2\varepsilon^2/\sigma^2=k^2ε2/σ2=k2, not k. The conclusion is a lower bound on the area: Area=1−P((X−μ)2/σ2≥k2)≥1−1/k2\text{Area}=1-\mathbb P\big((X-\mu)^2/\sigma^2\ge k^2\big)\ge1-1/k^2Area=1−P((X−μ)2/σ2≥k2)≥1−1/k2.

Risk illustration (made-up numbers). A daily return with mean 0 and SD 1%. Chebyshev guarantees P(∣R∣≥3%)≤1/9≈11%\mathbb P(|R|\ge3\%)\le1/9\approx11\%P(∣R∣≥3%)≤1/9≈11% for any distribution. If returns were normal the figure would be about 0.27%. The gap is the price of assuming nothing about tails; it is why distribution-free risk limits look conservative, and why fat-tailed data can sit much closer to the Chebyshev bound than to the normal figure.

Averages concentrate when their variance vanishes

For IID XiX_iXi​ with mean μ and variance σ2<∞\sigma^2<\inftyσ2<∞, Lecture 4 gave Var(Xˉn)=σ2/n\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/nVar(Xˉn​)=σ2/n. Apply Chebyshev to Xˉn\bar X_nXˉn​ (its mean is μ):

P(∣Xˉn−μ∣≥ε)≤Var(Xˉn)ε2=σ2nε2 ⟶ 0\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\varepsilon)\le\frac{\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)}{\varepsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\ \longrightarrow\ 0P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣≥ε)≤ε2Var(Xˉn​)​=nε2σ2​ ⟶ 0

This is a proof of a weak law of large numbers under finite variance (stated formally in Lecture 6).

  1. P(∣Xˉn−μ∣≥0.5)≤16n(0.5)2=64n\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge0.5)\le\dfrac{16}{n(0.5)^2}=\dfrac{64}{n}P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣≥0.5)≤n(0.5)216​=n64​.
  2. Require 64/n≤0.1064/n\le0.1064/n≤0.10 ⇒ n≥640n\ge640n≥640.
  3. Does the bound give the actual probability? No. It is a guarantee valid for every distribution with variance 16; the true probability is usually much smaller. If the data were normal, Xˉ640\bar X_{640}Xˉ640​ has SD 4/640≈0.1584/\sqrt{640}\approx0.1584/640​≈0.158, so the true probability is about P(∣Z∣≥3.16)≈0.0016\mathbb P(|Z|\ge3.16)\approx0.0016P(∣Z∣≥3.16)≈0.0016.

Convergence in probability

Xn→pXifP(∣Xn−X∣>ε)⟶0  for every ε>0X_n\xrightarrow{p}X\quad\text{if}\quad \mathbb P(|X_n-X|>\varepsilon)\longrightarrow0\ \ \text{for every }\varepsilon>0Xn​p​XifP(∣Xn​−X∣>ε)⟶0  for every ε>0
  • Order of quantifiers: fix the tolerance ε first, then let n grow. For every fixed positive tolerance, the probability of exceeding it must tend to zero.
  • The limit X may be random or a constant; in estimation it is usually a constant θ.

Consistency

The estimand θ is the quantity of interest. An estimator TnT_nTn​ of θ is consistent if

Tn→pθT_n\xrightarrow{p}\thetaTn​p​θ
  • It is a property of the estimator under a sequence of sampling models.
  • What it does not say: no rate, no small-sample guarantee, and no protection if the model or sampling assumptions fail (misspecification, selection).

Self-study recap: a small bias can coexist with consistency. Tn=Xˉn+1/nT_n=\bar X_n+1/nTn​=Xˉn​+1/n with IID finite-variance data.

  1. E[Tn]=μ+1/n≠μ\mathbb E[T_n]=\mu+1/n\ne\muE[Tn​]=μ+1/n=μ: biased at every n.
  2. Xˉn→pμ\bar X_n\xrightarrow{p}\muXˉn​p​μ (previous section) and 1/n→01/n\to01/n→0, so Tn→pμT_n\xrightarrow{p}\muTn​p​μ: but consistent.

Consistency is about the limiting error, not exact unbiasedness at each n.

Convergence in distribution

Xn→dXifFXn(x)→FX(x) at every continuity point x of FXX_n\xrightarrow{d}X\quad\text{if}\quad F_{X_n}(x)\to F_X(x)\ \text{at every continuity point } x \text{ of } F_XXn​d​XifFXn​​(x)→FX​(x) at every continuity point x of FX​

The CDFs become the same.

Why only continuity points? Take the constants Xn=1/nX_n=1/nXn​=1/n and X=0X=0X=0. Obviously Xn→0X_n\to0Xn​→0 in every sense. But at the jump x = 0, FXn(0)=P(1/n≤0)=0F_{X_n}(0)=\mathbb P(1/n\le0)=0FXn​​(0)=P(1/n≤0)=0 for every n, while FX(0)=1F_X(0)=1FX​(0)=1. Demanding convergence at x = 0 would wrongly reject a sequence that clearly converges. At every other x the CDFs do converge, so excluding the jump points gives the right answer.

Note that this sequence does converge in distribution to 0: x = 0 is a discontinuity point of FXF_XFX​, so it is excluded from the check. The example justifies the “continuity points” clause; it does not show failure.

Same distribution, different random variable

Second example (slide). Every Xn∼Bernoulli(1/2)X_n\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1/2)Xn​∼Bernoulli(1/2) and X∼Bernoulli(1/2)X\sim\text{Bernoulli}(1/2)X∼Bernoulli(1/2) independent of them. Convergence in distribution need not mean the variables are close. It compares laws; the variables need not even live on the same probability space.

Almost-sure convergence

Xn→a.s.XifP({ω:Xn(ω)→X(ω)})=1X_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X\quad\text{if}\quad\mathbb P\big(\{\omega: X_n(\omega)\to X(\omega)\}\big)=1Xn​a.s.​XifP({ω:Xn​(ω)→X(ω)})=1

The implication diagram has one direction

Xn→a.s.X ⟹ Xn→pX ⟹ Xn→dXX_n\xrightarrow{a.s.}X\ \Longrightarrow\ X_n\xrightarrow{p}X\ \Longrightarrow\ X_n\xrightarrow{d}XXn​a.s.​X ⟹ Xn​p​X ⟹ Xn​d​X

Which modes hold for Z/n?

Large samples do not repair wrong targets or assumptions

Large n does not guarantee accurate estimation. It does not repair:

  • a sample drawn from the wrong population;
  • persistent dependence that prevents averaging (Lecture 4, Activity 4.3);
  • an estimator converging to a value other than the estimand;
  • a limiting theorem used without its moment or regularity conditions.

Large-sample results still require the model assumptions to hold.

Consistency does not require variance to vanish

Let Tn=nT_n=nTn​=n with probability n−2n^{-2}n−2 and Tn=0T_n=0Tn​=0 otherwise. The target is 0.

  1. Consistency. For fixed ε > 0 and n > ε, ∣Tn∣>ε|T_n|>\varepsilon∣Tn​∣>ε only when Tn=nT_n=nTn​=n: P(∣Tn∣>ε)=n−2→0\mathbb P(|T_n|>\varepsilon)=n^{-2}\to0P(∣Tn​∣>ε)=n−2→0, so Tn→p0T_n\xrightarrow{p}0Tn​p​0.
  2. Mean. E[Tn]=n⋅n−2=n−1\mathbb E[T_n]=n\cdot n^{-2}=n^{-1}E[Tn​]=n⋅n−2=n−1.
  3. Variance. E[Tn2]=n2⋅n−2=1\mathbb E[T_n^2]=n^2\cdot n^{-2}=1E[Tn2​]=n2⋅n−2=1, so Var(Tn)=1−n−2→1\mathrm{Var}(T_n)=1-n^{-2}\to1Var(Tn​)=1−n−2→1.
  • Rare large errors keep the variance from vanishing even though large errors become ever less likely.
  • Converse direction: Var(Tn)→0\mathrm{Var}(T_n)\to0Var(Tn​)→0 proves consistency only if the mean also approaches the target (bias → 0). Proof via Chebyshev applied around the mean plus the bias shrinking. So “bias → 0 and variance → 0” is sufficient, not necessary.

Summary

  • Markov and Chebyshev turn moments into probability bounds.
  • Vanishing variance plus a mean approaching the target establish consistency.
  • Consistency means convergence in probability to the estimand.
  • Convergence in distribution means convergence of CDFs at continuity points of the limiting CDF.
  • Almost sure ⇒ probability ⇒ distribution.
  • Next: the LLN describes where sample averages go; the CLT describes the limiting distribution of their scaled errors.

Traps

  • Applying Markov to a variable that can be negative.
  • Writing the Markov or Chebyshev event the wrong way round (≤\le≤ inside instead of ≥\ge≥).
  • Substituting ε=kσ\varepsilon=k\sigmaε=kσ and getting threshold k instead of k2k^2k2.
  • Reading a Chebyshev bound as the actual probability (Activity 5.1).
  • Saying Xn=UX_n=UXn​=U when only the distributions match (Activity 5.2).
  • Concluding “no convergence in distribution” from failure at a jump point of FXF_XFX​.
  • Reversing an implication arrow (d ⇒ p is false unless the limit is a constant).
  • Claiming a consistent estimator must have vanishing variance, or that vanishing variance alone gives consistency.
  • Writing “with probability 0” for “with probability one”.
