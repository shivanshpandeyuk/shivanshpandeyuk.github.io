Cheat sheet

  • LLN (where does it go?): IID, E∣Xi∣<∞\mathbb E|X_i|<\inftyE∣Xi​∣<∞ ⇒ Xˉn→pμ\bar X_n\xrightarrow{p}\muXˉn​p​μ. With finite variance, Chebyshev proves it: P(∣Xˉn−μ∣>ε)≤σ2/(nε2)\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\varepsilon)\le\sigma^2/(n\varepsilon^2)P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣>ε)≤σ2/(nε2).

  • Rate: error is of order 1/n1/\sqrt n1/n​; halving the SE needs 4× the data.
  • Fixed vs shrinking tolerance: P(∣Xˉn−μ∣<σ)→1\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|<\sigma)\to1P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣<σ)→1 (LLN); P(∣Xˉn−μ∣<σ/n)→2Φ(1)−1≈0.6827\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|<\sigma/\sqrt n)\to2\Phi(1)-1\approx0.6827P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣<σ/n​)→2Φ(1)−1≈0.6827 (CLT).

  • Exact t: normal IID data ⇒ n(Xˉn−μ)/Sn∼tn−1\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/S_n\sim t_{n-1}n​(Xˉn​−μ)/Sn​∼tn−1​ for every n ≥ 2. tν=Z/U/νt_\nu=Z/\sqrt{U/\nu}tν​=Z/U/ν​, Z⊥U∼χν2Z\perp U\sim\chi^2_\nuZ⊥U∼χν2​.

The LLN and CLT answer different questions

For IID observations with mean μ and 0<σ2<∞0<\sigma^2<\infty0<σ2<∞:

Xˉn→pμ⏟LLN: where does it go?andn(Xˉn−μ)σ→dN(0,1)⏟CLT: how does it fluctuate?\underbrace{\bar X_n\xrightarrow{p}\mu}_{\text{LLN: where does it go?}}\qquad\text{and}\qquad\underbrace{\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1)}_{\text{CLT: how does it fluctuate?}}LLN: where does it go?Xˉn​p​μ​​andCLT: how does it fluctuate?σn​(Xˉn​−μ)​d​N(0,1)​​
  • LLN: the sample mean converges in probability to the population mean.
  • Consistency gives the limiting value; a limiting distribution describes the scaled estimation error.

Note that the standardised sample mean converges in distribution to N(0, 1), not to μ. The unscaled Xˉn\bar X_nXˉn​ goes to μ (LLN); once μ is subtracted and the result multiplied by n/σ\sqrt n/\sigman​/σ, the error is blown up to a fixed scale and its shape tends to the standard normal.

A weak law makes the sample mean consistent

Statement. If X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\dotsX1​,X2​,… are IID with E[Xi]=μ\mathbb E[X_i]=\muE[Xi​]=μ and finite variance, then Xˉn→pμ\bar X_n\xrightarrow{p}\muXˉn​p​μ, i.e. Xˉn−μ→p0\bar X_n-\mu\xrightarrow{p}0Xˉn​−μp​0.

Proof (Chebyshev, from Lecture 5).

  1. E[Xˉn]=μ\mathbb E[\bar X_n]=\muE[Xˉn​]=μ and Var(Xˉn)=σ2/n\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\sigma^2/nVar(Xˉn​)=σ2/n (Lecture 4, uses independence).

  2. Chebyshev applied to Xˉn\bar X_nXˉn​ with tolerance ε:

    P(∣Xˉn−μ∣>ε)≤σ2/nε2=σ2nε2⟶0\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|>\varepsilon)\le\frac{\sigma^2/n}{\varepsilon^2}=\frac{\sigma^2}{n\varepsilon^2}\longrightarrow0P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣>ε)≤ε2σ2/n​=nε2σ2​⟶0
  • Notation: write Var(Xˉn)\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)Var(Xˉn​) for σ2/n\sigma^2/nσ2/n, since S2S^2S2 is reserved for the sample variance.
  • Weaker condition: for IID data, E∣Xi∣<∞\mathbb E|X_i|<\inftyE∣Xi​∣<∞ is enough for the LLN. Finite variance is only needed for this short Chebyshev proof.
  • No law works without conditions on dependence and tails (see the Cauchy and dependence sections below).

The LLN gives concentration, not a normal approximation

Xˉn→pμ\bar X_n\xrightarrow{p}\muXˉn​p​μ says that fixed errors become unlikely. It does not give:

  • the shape of the sampling distribution;
  • the rate at which the error shrinks;
  • probabilities for shrinking error tolerances;
  • whether a normal approximation is accurate at the observed n.

The classical CLT

If X1,X2,…X_1,X_2,\dotsX1​,X2​,… are IID with E[Xi]=μ\mathbb E[X_i]=\muE[Xi​]=μ and 0<Var(Xi)=σ2<∞0<\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2<\infty0<Var(Xi​)=σ2<∞:

n(Xˉn−μ)σ→dN(0,1)⟺Xˉn ∼˙ N(μ,σ2n) for large n\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1) \qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad \bar X_n\ \dot\sim\ \mathcal N\Big(\mu,\frac{\sigma^2}{n}\Big)\ \text{for large } nσn​(Xˉn​−μ)​d​N(0,1)⟺Xˉn​ ∼˙ N(μ,nσ2​) for large n
  • The first statement is the convergence theorem; the ”≈” version is the approximation it licenses for practical use.
  • The standardised form subtracts the mean and divides by the standard error σ/n\sigma/\sqrt nσ/n​: σ is the SD of the observations, σ/n\sigma/\sqrt nσ/n​ the SE of the sample mean, and the standardised error is (Xˉn−μ)/(σ/n)(\bar X_n-\mu)/(\sigma/\sqrt n)(Xˉn​−μ)/(σ/n​).
  • Assumptions: IID, finite and positive variance. No normality of the data is needed.

Why √n is the natural scale

  1. Var(Xˉn−μ)=σ2/n\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n-\mu)=\sigma^2/nVar(Xˉn​−μ)=σ2/n, so the unscaled error shrinks like 1/n1/\sqrt n1/n​ and its distribution collapses to a point.

  2. Multiplying by n\sqrt nn​ keeps the variance fixed at σ², giving a non-degenerate limit (the limiting distribution has positive variance):

    n(Xˉn−μ)→dN(0,σ2)\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2)n​(Xˉn​−μ)d​N(0,σ2)
  • Multiply by a faster rate and the variance explodes; by a slower one and it still collapses. n\sqrt nn​ is the only scale that gives a finite, non-zero spread.
  • Interpretation: SE ∝ 1/n1/\sqrt n1/n​, so halving the standard error needs about four times as many independent observations.

Fixed versus shrinking error tolerances

Activity 6.1. IID, mean μ, variance 0<σ2<∞0<\sigma^2<\infty0<σ2<∞. Limits of P(∣Xˉn−μ∣<σ)\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|<\sigma)P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣<σ) and P(∣Xˉn−μ∣<σ/n)\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|<\sigma/\sqrt n)P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣<σ/n​)?

  1. Fixed tolerance σ. By the LLN, P(∣Xˉn−μ∣≥σ)→0\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|\ge\sigma)\to0P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣≥σ)→0, so P(∣Xˉn−μ∣<σ)→1\mathbb P(|\bar X_n-\mu|<\sigma)\to1P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣<σ)→1.

  2. Shrinking tolerance σ/√n. Rewrite in standardised form:

    P(∣Xˉn−μ∣<σn)=P(∣n(Xˉn−μ)σ∣<1) ⟶ P(∣Z∣<1)=2Φ(1)−1≈0.6827\mathbb P\Big(|\bar X_n-\mu|<\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}\Big)=\mathbb P\Big(\Big|\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{\sigma}\Big|<1\Big)\ \longrightarrow\ \mathbb P(|Z|<1)=2\Phi(1)-1\approx0.6827P(∣Xˉn​−μ∣<n​σ​)=P(​σn​(Xˉn​−μ)​​<1) ⟶ P(∣Z∣<1)=2Φ(1)−1≈0.6827

    by the CLT (Z∼N(0,1)Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)Z∼N(0,1)).

So they do not both tend to one. Consistency alone says nothing about tolerances that shrink with n.

Watch three points here. The activity does not assume normality, only mean μ and finite variance, which is why the answer uses the CLT. The LLN gives no rate; the √n rate comes from the CLT. And it is P(∣Z∣<1)=2Φ(1)−1\mathbb P(|Z|<1)=2\Phi(1)-1P(∣Z∣<1)=2Φ(1)−1, whereas P(Z<1)=Φ(1)≈0.841\mathbb P(Z<1)=\Phi(1)\approx0.841P(Z<1)=Φ(1)≈0.841.

Standard errors

SD refers to the population, the original random variable; SE refers to sampling, the estimator.

SE(Tn)=Var(Tn) under the sampling model,SE(Xˉn)=σn\mathrm{SE}(T_n)=\sqrt{\mathrm{Var}(T_n)}\ \text{under the sampling model},\qquad \mathrm{SE}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma}{\sqrt n}SE(Tn​)=Var(Tn​)​ under the sampling model,SE(Xˉn​)=n​σ​

The SE is not the SD of the raw observations, and it is not the realised estimation error xˉ−μ\bar x-\muxˉ−μ (which is unknown).

Standardise using the standard error

Activity 6.2. IID with mean 10 and variance 9. Approximate P(Xˉ100>10.6)\mathbb P(\bar X_{100}>10.6)P(Xˉ100​>10.6). Is the SE 3, 0.3 or 0.03? Is the result exact?

  1. SD of observations =9=3=\sqrt9=3=9​=3. SE of Xˉ100=3/100=0.3\bar X_{100}=3/\sqrt{100}=0.3Xˉ100​=3/100​=0.3.

  2. Standardise:

    10.6−103/100=0.60.3=2\frac{10.6-10}{3/\sqrt{100}}=\frac{0.6}{0.3}=23/100​10.6−10​=0.30.6​=2

  3. CLT: P(Xˉ100>10.6)≈P(Z>2)=1−Φ(2)≈0.0228\mathbb P(\bar X_{100}>10.6)\approx\mathbb P(Z>2)=1-\Phi(2)\approx0.0228P(Xˉ100​>10.6)≈P(Z>2)=1−Φ(2)≈0.0228.

  4. Not exact. IID and finite variance only give a limit theorem; nothing guarantees accuracy at n = 100.

Note that the question gives only the mean and variance, not normality, so Xˉ100 ∼˙ N(10,9/100)\bar X_{100}\ \dot\sim\ \mathcal N(10,9/100)Xˉ100​ ∼˙ N(10,9/100) is approximate (by the CLT), not exact. The denominator in the standardisation is the SE, 0.3, on both sides.

The CLT for binomial counts

If Kn∼Bin(n,p)K_n\sim\text{Bin}(n,p)Kn​∼Bin(n,p) with fixed 0<p<10<p<10<p<1, then Kn=∑i=1nXiK_n=\sum_{i=1}^nX_iKn​=∑i=1n​Xi​ with IID Xi∼Bernoulli(p)X_i\sim\text{Bernoulli}(p)Xi​∼Bernoulli(p), which have mean p and variance p(1−p)p(1-p)p(1−p). Hence

Kn−npnp(1−p)→dN(0,1),Kn ∼˙ N(np, np(1−p))\frac{K_n-np}{\sqrt{np(1-p)}}\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1),\qquad K_n\ \dot\sim\ \mathcal N\big(np,\ np(1-p)\big)np(1−p)​Kn​−np​d​N(0,1),Kn​ ∼˙ N(np, np(1−p))
  • Xˉn=Kn/n\bar X_n=K_n/nXˉn​=Kn​/n is a sample mean. Dividing numerator and denominator by n gives exactly the CLT for Xˉn\bar X_nXˉn​ with μ = p, σ² = p(1 − p).
  • Quality deteriorates when np or n(1 − p) is small: these are the expected numbers of successes and of failures.

Note that np is the expected number of successes and n(1 − p) the expected number of failures; the variance is np(1 − p). The standardisation of Xˉn\bar X_nXˉn​ needs the √n: (Xˉn−p)/p(1−p)/n(\bar X_n-p)\big/\sqrt{p(1-p)/n}(Xˉn​−p)/p(1−p)/n​, which equals the expression above.

Continuity correction (self-study recap)

A count takes integer values; the normal is continuous. Treat each integer k as the interval (k−12,k+12)(k-\tfrac12,k+\tfrac12)(k−21​,k+21​).

Example. S∼Bin(200,0.30)S\sim\text{Bin}(200,0.30)S∼Bin(200,0.30): ES=60\mathbb E S=60ES=60, VarS=200(0.3)(0.7)=42\mathrm{Var}S=200(0.3)(0.7)=42VarS=200(0.3)(0.7)=42, SD=42≈6.48\mathrm{SD}=\sqrt{42}\approx6.48SD=42​≈6.48.

  1. {S≥70}={S>69.5}\{S\ge70\}=\{S>69.5\}{S≥70}={S>69.5} on the continuous scale.
  2. z=(69.5−60)/6.48≈1.47z=(69.5-60)/6.48\approx1.47z=(69.5−60)/6.48≈1.47.
  3. P(S≥70)≈1−Φ(1.47)≈0.071\mathbb P(S\ge70)\approx1-\Phi(1.47)\approx0.071P(S≥70)≈1−Φ(1.47)≈0.071.

Check: the exact binomial value is 0.073. Without the correction, (70−60)/6.48=1.54(70-60)/6.48=1.54(70−60)/6.48=1.54 gives about 0.061, noticeably worse.

Is n = 100 enough?

Activity 6.3. A∼Bin(100,0.5)A\sim\text{Bin}(100,0.5)A∼Bin(100,0.5) and B∼Bin(100,0.01)B\sim\text{Bin}(100,0.01)B∼Bin(100,0.01). “Both have n = 100, so both are well approximated by a normal.” Agree?

  1. A: expected successes 50, failures 50. Symmetric; normal approximation plausible.
  2. B: expected successes 100×0.01=1100\times0.01=1100×0.01=1, failures 99. Very skewed, with P(B=0)=0.99100≈0.366\mathbb P(B=0)=0.99^{100}\approx0.366P(B=0)=0.99100≈0.366. A normal centred at 1 with SD ≈ 0.99 puts substantial mass below 0, which is impossible for a count.
  3. A has the better normal approximation. Sample size alone does not determine approximation quality; the expected counts do.

This sort of approximation is good at the centre and poor at the tails. The slides show this with Bin(n, 0.1) for n = 10, 100, 1000 (np = 1, 10, 100): central agreement improves quickly, extreme-tail accuracy can stay poor.

Studentisation: replacing the unknown σ

Usually σ is unknown. Replace it with the sample SD:

Tn=n(Xˉn−μ)Sn,Sn2=1n−1∑i=1n(Xi−Xˉn)2, n≥2T_n=\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{S_n},\qquad S_n^2=\frac1{n-1}\sum_{i=1}^n(X_i-\bar X_n)^2,\ n\ge2Tn​=Sn​n​(Xˉn​−μ)​,Sn2​=n−11​i=1∑n​(Xi​−Xˉn​)2, n≥2
  • Under the CLT conditions, Sn→pσ>0S_n\xrightarrow{p}\sigma>0Sn​p​σ>0 and Tn→dN(0,1)T_n\xrightarrow{d}\mathcal N(0,1)Tn​d​N(0,1). (Set Tn=0T_n=0Tn​=0 when Sn=0S_n=0Sn​=0; that event has probability tending to zero.)
  • The substitution is justified by Slutsky’s theorem (Lecture 7).
  • Replacing σ by SnS_nSn​ introduces more variability, so convergence to the standard normal is slower: for small n, TnT_nTn​ has heavier tails than N(0, 1). For normal data that extra variability is exactly the t-distribution.

Normal samples give an exact Student t (self-study recap)

If X1,…,Xn∼iidN(μ,σ2)X_1,\dots,X_n\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)X1​,…,Xn​∼iidN(μ,σ2), n ≥ 2, σ² > 0, then exactly

n(Xˉn−μ)Sn∼tn−1\frac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{S_n}\sim t_{n-1}Sn​n​(Xˉn​−μ)​∼tn−1​

Where it comes from (links to Lecture 4).

  1. Z=n(Xˉn−μ)/σ∼N(0,1)Z=\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma\sim\mathcal N(0,1)Z=n​(Xˉn​−μ)/σ∼N(0,1).
  2. U=(n−1)Sn2/σ2∼χn−12U=(n-1)S_n^2/\sigma^2\sim\chi^2_{n-1}U=(n−1)Sn2​/σ2∼χn−12​, and Xˉn⊥ ⁣ ⁣ ⁣⊥Sn2\bar X_n\perp\!\!\!\perp S_n^2Xˉn​⊥⊥Sn2​.
  3. Divide: ZU/(n−1)=n(Xˉn−μ)/σSn/σ=n(Xˉn−μ)Sn\dfrac{Z}{\sqrt{U/(n-1)}}=\dfrac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)/\sigma}{S_n/\sigma}=\dfrac{\sqrt n(\bar X_n-\mu)}{S_n}U/(n−1)​Z​=Sn​/σn​(Xˉn​−μ)/σ​=Sn​n​(Xˉn​−μ)​. The σ cancels.
  4. By definition tνt_\nutν​ is the law of Z/U/νZ/\sqrt{U/\nu}Z/U/ν​ with Z⊥UZ\perp UZ⊥U, so this is tn−1t_{n-1}tn−1​.

This quantity has a t distribution regardless of μ and σ (it is pivotal). It is no longer N(0, 1) but a t; as n → ∞, t→N(0,1)t\to\mathcal N(0,1)t→N(0,1) anyway.

A valid CLT can still be a poor approximation

  • Skewness, heavy tails (with finite variance) and rare events can leave the finite-sample distribution far from normal.
  • Practical check: there is no universal threshold for “large enough”. Quality depends on the underlying distribution and on how far into the tail the estimate goes. Rules of thumb exist, but only as rules of thumb.
  • Central probabilities may be accurate while extreme-tail probabilities remain poor.

Cauchy averages do not stabilise

Standard Cauchy density f(x)=1π(1+x2)f(x)=\dfrac{1}{\pi(1+x^2)}f(x)=π(1+x2)1​, x∈Rx\in\mathbb Rx∈R. If X1,…,XnX_1,\dots,X_nX1​,…,Xn​ are IID Cauchy(0, 1), then

Xˉn∼Cauchy(0,1)for every n\bar X_n\sim\text{Cauchy}(0,1)\quad\text{for every } nXˉn​∼Cauchy(0,1)for every n
  • No finite mean, but the median is 0. The sample mean has the same Cauchy distribution, so it is a useless estimator of the centre of a Cauchy distribution.
  • The tails decay like 1/x21/x^21/x2, so E∣X∣=∫∣x∣f(x) dx\mathbb E|X|=\int|x|f(x)\,dxE∣X∣=∫∣x∣f(x)dx diverges: no mean, no variance, and both the LLN and the classical CLT assumptions fail. More observations do not automatically make an average well behaved.
  • The sample median is consistent for the centre here, which is why robust estimators matter with heavy tails.

Dependence can stop the variance shrinking

If Var(Xi)=σ2\mathrm{Var}(X_i)=\sigma^2Var(Xi​)=σ2 and Cov(Xi,Xj)=γ>0\mathrm{Cov}(X_i,X_j)=\gamma>0Cov(Xi​,Xj​)=γ>0 for i≠ji\ne ji=j (from Lecture 4):

Var(Xˉn)=σ2n+n−1nγ ⟶ γ\mathrm{Var}(\bar X_n)=\frac{\sigma^2}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}\gamma\ \longrightarrow\ \gammaVar(Xˉn​)=nσ2​+nn−1​γ ⟶ γ
  • The covariance does not fall out as n → ∞.
  • The IID formula σ/n\sigma/\sqrt nσ/n​ would go to zero while the true SE tends to γ>0\sqrt\gamma>0γ​>0. Clustered and time-series data need dependence-aware theory.

Conditions checklist

  1. What statistic is changing with n?
  2. Are the observations independent (or weakly dependent, or clustered)?
  3. Are they identically distributed, or is there a trend?
  4. Does the required mean or variance exist?
  5. Is the approximation used in the centre or the far tail?

State the theorem and the conditions together.

Summary

  • The LLN makes averages consistent under suitable conditions.
  • The CLT gives a standard normal limit for the standardised sample mean.
  • Standard errors describe estimator variability under repeated sampling.
  • Studentisation replaces the unknown σ by a consistent estimate.
  • Without finite variance or independence, the classical IID CLT need not hold.
  • Next (Lecture 7): Slutsky and the delta method transfer limiting results to new statistics.

Traps

  • Saying the standardised mean converges to μ (it converges in distribution to N(0, 1)).
  • Attributing a √n rate to the LLN.
  • Using the SD of the observations (3) where the SE (0.3) belongs.
  • Writing ∼\sim∼ (exact) when the CLT only gives ∼˙\dot\sim∼˙.
  • P(Z<1)\mathbb P(Z<1)P(Z<1) versus P(∣Z∣<1)\mathbb P(|Z|<1)P(∣Z∣<1).
  • Trusting a normal approximation because n is large while np is small (Bin(100, 0.01)).
  • Confusing n(1 − p) (expected failures) with the variance np(1 − p).
  • Using the t-distribution as exact for non-normal data, or the IID SE for dependent data.
  • Applying the LLN or CLT to Cauchy-type heavy tails.
